\chapter{Optical validation}
\label{ch:validation}

A datasheet that closes on a quiet bench is not a product. \emph{Validation}
reduces uncertainty about whether a link meets its requirements across the
temperatures, hosts, connectors, production spread, and lifetime the fleet will
actually see. Passing tests is an output, not the purpose. This chapter walks the
ladder from a single device to a deployed fleet, the engineering question at
each stage, module and system bring-up under production-like corners, and the
hypothesis-driven debug method the work demands.

Debugging asks which margin ledger was exhausted. Qualification asks how much
margin remains after the expected stresses. Both are uncertainty reduction that
ends in a decision (\Cref{app:decision-trees}).

\section{The validation ladder}
\label{sec:validation-workflow}

Optical programs fail in the same places again and again: a part that looks good
in characterization but cannot bring up on a production host, or a module that
passes acceptance test procedure (\term{ATP}\firstterm{ATP}) and then unlocks
under neighbor heat. The ladder below is a decision framework, not a test menu.
Each stage answers one question the previous stage could not answer. Skipping a
rung does not save time. It moves the escape into a later, more expensive stage.

Requirements sit above the first gate: reach, lane rate, hosts, BER/FEC target,
power and thermal envelope, lifetime, and production volume. Without those
constraints, later measurements have no pass criterion
(\Cref{sec:interview-validation-ladder}).

\subsection{Stage 1: Bring-up}
\label{sec:ladder-bringup}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Does the product operate at all on a known-good host? Bring-up asks whether
power, management, light, timing recovery, and a usable error rate exist before
anyone argues about margin.

\paragraph{Uncertainty removed.}
Before bring-up you do not know whether a failure is integration (seat, cable,
firmware, host) or product physics. After bring-up you know the unit can emit,
receive, and pass data under controlled conditions.

\paragraph{Activities.}
Power the module or engine. Confirm management presence and state progression
through the Common Management Interface Specification
(\term{CMIS}\firstterm{CMIS}) state machine to a ready state. Enable transmit
only when commanded. Verify first light and received power. Confirm clock and
data recovery (\term{CDR}\firstterm{CDR}) lock. Measure pre-FEC bit error
ratio (\term{pre-FEC BER}\firstterm{pre-FEC BER}) on a golden host. Details
and fail branches live in \Cref{sec:bringup,tab:bringup-checklist}.

\paragraph{Measurements and evidence.}
CMIS presence and ready state confirm the management path before you blame
optics. CDR lock confirms the receiver can recover timing from the incoming
stream; without stable lock, later BER work may only restate an integration
fault. Pre-FEC BER is the first system health number: it is the error rate
before FEC cleans the link, so it still reveals optical and electrical margin.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} the unit reaches ready state, emits and receives light in
class, holds CDR lock, and shows a usable pre-FEC BER on the golden host with
named reference planes.

\paragraph{Decision unlocked.}
Continue into characterization, or stop and debug integration (seat, power,
firmware, fiber, host).

\paragraph{Risk if skipped.}
You may spend weeks optimizing TDECQ or life models on a part that never
reliably links in a real chassis.

\subsection{Stage 2: Characterization}
\label{sec:ladder-characterization}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
How does the product behave across temperature, voltage, optical return loss
(\term{ORL}\firstterm{ORL}), aging proxies, lanes, and units? Characterization
maps the response surface. It does not yet prove fleet survival.

\paragraph{Uncertainty removed.}
Before characterization you know one corner works. After it you know how
distributions move with stress variables and which ledgers (power, noise,
timing, spectral, control) are thin.

\paragraph{Activities.}
Sweep case or junction temperature and supply. Stress ORL where reflections
matter. Compare units and lots. On the transmitter path, measure transmitter
and dispersion eye closure quaternary (\term{TDECQ}\firstterm{TDECQ}), outer
optical modulation amplitude, extinction ratio, and level linearity. On
components with access, measure light--current--voltage (\term{LIV}\firstterm{LIV}),
relative intensity noise (\term{RIN}\firstterm{RIN}), and side-mode suppression
ratio (\term{SMSR}\firstterm{SMSR}). Close a signed link-budget ledger
(\Cref{sec:tdecq,sec:link-budget,sec:laser-params}).

\paragraph{Measurements and evidence.}
TDECQ scores transmitter quality after a reference equalizer; a rise with
stable average power points at bandwidth, linearity, or bias, not simple loss.
Changes in the LIV baseline can support a physical-aging hypothesis, while a
substantially healthy LIV combined with recovery after recalibration supports
setpoint or control drift. LIV alone does not always perform a definitive
separation. RIN under controlled ORL reveals feedback-sensitive floors. SMSR
checks single-mode purity as temperature or age changes. Sensitivity and
BER-versus-power waterfalls show whether the receiver path shifts or floors
(\Cref{sec:interview-waterfall}).

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} you have mapped population behavior versus the required
corners, named the thin ledgers, and decided whether the design needs derate
or redesign before loaded-fleet work.

\paragraph{Decision unlocked.}
Proceed to margin and interoperability, derate the envelope, or redesign.

\paragraph{Risk if skipped.}
A hero sample that worked at 25~$^\circ$C becomes the silent assumption for
life and volume. Corner failures then appear as ``surprises'' in DVT.

\subsection{Stage 3: Margin and interoperability}
\label{sec:ladder-margin-interop}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Does remaining margin survive realistic system variation: chassis thermal load,
live host supplies, dirty fiber, neighbor traffic, cable plant, and the target
host or peer module?

\paragraph{Uncertainty removed.}
Characterization maps the part. This stage asks whether that map still holds
when the surrounding system is hostile in production-like ways
(\Cref{sec:prod-corners,sec:laser-margin-erosion}).

\paragraph{Activities.}
Run production-representative corners: module in target sled airflow, host
rails with SerDes traffic, controlled contamination or ORL stress, production
fiber length and bend radius, ELS hot-swap if the architecture promises it,
adjacent modules at full load, and at least one non-golden host or second-
source peer when multi-source is claimed.

\paragraph{Measurements and evidence.}
Compare pre-FEC BER, telemetry, retrain count, and control headroom at the
failing corner against the characterization baseline. An optical eye, when
used, is measured externally unless an internal eye-monitor is explicitly
named (\Cref{sec:tree-blackbox-access}). Interop failures often land in CMIS,
media type, or electrical eye rather than laser physics.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} loaded corners and target hosts close with acceptable
remaining margin, or when a documented restriction defines where the product
may ship.

\paragraph{Decision unlocked.}
Approve the fleet corner, restrict deployment, or send the design back.

\paragraph{Risk if skipped.}
Quiet-bench margin evaporates in the first rack. Field tickets then look like
random laser failures when the real escape was an untested corner.

\subsection{Stage 4: Stress qualification}
\label{sec:ladder-stress}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Will the product survive its intended life under a named wear-out or
environmental mechanism, not merely pass a short burn-in?

\paragraph{Uncertainty removed.}
Functional and margin stages do not answer lifetime. Stress qualification
separates infant mortality and wear-out risk from operating-point mistakes
(\Cref{sec:gr468,sec:ic-reliability}).

\paragraph{Activities.}
Run high-temperature operating life (\term{HTOL}\firstterm{HTOL}), temperature
cycling, humidity where claimed, electrostatic discharge
(\term{ESD}\firstterm{ESD}) robustness, and connector mating cycles as required
by the product class (often GR-468 / GR-1221 for optics and JESD47-class
thinking for ICs). Name the mechanism and justify activation energy $E_a$ when
you project FIT.

\paragraph{Measurements and evidence.}
Track the same customer-visible margins (power, BER/FEC, lock, wavelength)
before and after stress. LIV, SMSR, or dark checks on returns separate true
aging from calibration drift. A life claim without a mechanism is a narrative,
not evidence.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} the sample plan, mechanism, and projected life support the
requirements slice, or when you explicitly hold ship for life risk.

\paragraph{Decision unlocked.}
Accept life risk for the envelope, derate life or use conditions, or hold.

\paragraph{Risk if skipped.}
Early life failures concentrate by date code after volume ramp. Containment
then costs more than the omitted HTOL matrix.

\subsection{Stage 5: Production readiness}
\label{sec:ladder-production}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Can the supplier reproduce the qualified result at volume, with screens that
catch the escapes you care about?

\paragraph{Uncertainty removed.}
Qualification on engineering lots does not prove manufacturing control.
Production readiness asks whether yield, process control, and ATP coverage
survive lot-to-lot variation (\Cref{sec:hvm-test,sec:supplier-exec}).

\paragraph{Activities.}
Review multi-lot yield and statistical process control
(\term{SPC}\firstterm{SPC}). Correlate automated test equipment
(\term{ATE}\firstterm{ATE}) to bench truth. Freeze ATP limits that catch the
known escape paths. Complete first-article / FAIR gates and Design Validation
Test / Production Validation Test (\term{DVT}\firstterm{DVT} /
\term{PVT}\firstterm{PVT}) exits that match the requirements slice.

\paragraph{Measurements and evidence.}
Lot distributions for the same metrics used in characterization, guardbands
between ATP and field fail, and evidence that the ATP would have caught prior
escapes. Split supplier RMA codes so one vendor cannot hide inside a merged
bucket.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} multi-lot yield, ATP coverage, SPC stability, and FAIR
evidence support opening volume, or when you hold for process control.

\paragraph{Decision unlocked.}
Open volume, hold shipment, or demand corrective action before ramp.

\paragraph{Risk if skipped.}
A qualified hero process ships an uncontrolled second lot. Fleet triage then
becomes your de facto ATP.

\subsection{Stage 6: Controlled pilot and fleet monitoring}
\label{sec:ladder-fleet}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Were the qualification assumptions correct after real deployment, install
practice, and traffic mix?

\paragraph{Uncertainty removed.}
Lab corners never reproduce every rack. Pilot and fleet monitoring ask whether
remaining margin, escape rate, and cohort signatures match the model
(\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).

\paragraph{Activities.}
Run a limited population with enhanced telemetry and clear exit criteria.
Watch BER/FEC, retrains, power, temperature, lane behavior, and lot or site
cohorts. Preserve CMIS dumps before reseat. Feed signatures back into ATP and
design rules.

\paragraph{Measurements and evidence.}
Cohort rates, not single tickets. Time behavior (sudden versus creep).
Cool-down recovery versus permanent shift. Owner assignment uses the fleet
triage map in \Cref{tab:fleet-triage}.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} pilot exit criteria are met and named owners watch the
fleet metrics that would falsify the qual assumptions, or when you restrict
or reject based on observed risk.

\paragraph{Decision unlocked.}
Ship broadly, restrict, pause a supplier or lot, or reopen an earlier ladder
stage.

\paragraph{Risk if skipped.}
You learn the real escape mechanism from customer outage instead of from a
controlled pilot.

\subsection{Why the stages occur in this order}

Bring-up establishes that the setup and product fundamentally operate.
Characterization maps behavior and variation. Margin and interoperability
determine whether behavior survives realistic use. Stress qualification tests
projected lifetime under a named mechanism. Production readiness determines
whether the supplier can reproduce the result at volume. Pilot and fleet
monitoring check whether assumptions remain valid after deployment.

Later stages must not compensate for incomplete earlier stages. An HTOL pass
does not prove bring-up on the target host. An ATP correlation does not prove
remaining margin under neighbor heat. Treat each exit as evidence only for its
own question (\Cref{tab:ladder-gates,sec:tree-qualification}).

\subsection{Learning summary}
\label{sec:ladder-summary}

\begin{description}
\item[Bring-up] Does it operate?
\item[Characterization] How does it behave?
\item[Margin and interoperability] Does it survive realistic system variation?
\item[Stress qualification] Will it survive its intended life?
\item[Production readiness] Can it be built and screened repeatedly at volume?
\item[Pilot and fleet] Were the qualification assumptions correct in deployment?
\end{description}

\section{The core IM/DD measurements}

Once the ladder is clear, the measurement list is organized around isolation:
transmitter, channel, and receiver. That split is older than PAM4. Long before
TDECQ, field engineers learned that a dark link can be a dead laser, a dirty
connector, or a dead TIA, and that guessing which one burns hours. Bisecting those
three domains is still how you keep debug from turning into simultaneous retunes
of everything.

\subsection{Transmitter}

Start with the light leaving the faceplate or the CPO fiber array. For PAM4, the
headline metric is \term{TDECQ}\firstterm{TDECQ} (transmitter and dispersion eye
closure quaternary): a reference equalizer is applied to the captured eye and the
residual penalty is reported in dB (\Cref{sec:tdecq}). Alongside it you read
\term{OMA}\firstterm{OMA} (outer), extinction ratio, and \term{RLM}\firstterm{RLM}
(level linearity), plus wavelength, spectral width, and RIN with a bias-driver
versus feedback bisect (\Cref{sec:laser-params,sec:laser-drivers,sec:rin-values}).

What else you add depends on the transmitter style. Laser-bearing modules need
LIV, threshold, slope, SMSR, and chirp checks for DMLs
(\Cref{sec:laser-params,sec:dfb-eml}). External MZMs (TFLN or silicon) need EO
$S_{21}$, $V_\pi$, quadrature bias versus temperature, and driver-path eye
symmetry at baud (\Cref{sec:simzm,sec:tfln-mzm,sec:tdecq}). Microring banks need
resonance alignment, thermal tuning, neighbor crosstalk, and peaking-network EO
$S_{21}$ (\Cref{sec:siring,ch:wdm}). The point of the list is not completeness for
its own sake: it is knowing which instrument answers which hypothesis when the
eye closes.

\subsection{Channel}
\label{sec:optical-channel}

If the transmitter looks clean into a golden receiver and the link still fails,
the channel is next. Insertion loss from fiber, connectors, MUX/de-MUX
(\Cref{sec:wdm-hardware}), and on-chip coupling (\Cref{sec:simzm,sec:siring}) is
the first ledger line; plan about 1--3~dB per fiber interface. Chromatic
dispersion (\Cref{sec:chirp-dispersion}) matters more on FR-class SMF sweeps than
on short DR links. Optical return loss (ORL) is the quiet killer: reflections
back into the laser raise RIN and seed burst errors, which is why many DR/FR
modules still carry isolators while some CPO engines rely on design margin and
monitor photodiodes instead (\Cref{sec:rin-values,ch:lasers}). Fiber attach
(MPO/MTP, FAU, grating couplers) shows up as both yield and reliability
(\Cref{sec:photonic-packaging}).

\subsection{Receiver}

Receiver work asks whether the front-end can still decide bits at the OMA that
survives the channel. Measure sensitivity (minimum OMA for the target BER) and
stressed-receiver sensitivity with a calibrated stressor for margin
(\Cref{sec:secq}), plus overload before the TIA saturates. Underneath those
system numbers sit the photodiode/TIA pair: responsivity, bandwidth, and
input-referred noise (\Cref{sec:pd-tia,ch:models}).

\subsection{Link level}

Only after Tx, channel, and Rx each look sane do you trust a full-link verdict:
pre-FEC BER against the KP4 threshold (\Cref{sec:kp4}), post-FEC BER, FEC
symbol-error histograms, and a signed link-budget ledger from transmitter OMA to
receiver sensitivity with penalties and remaining margin. That ledger is the
document you argue from in DVT; the BER alone is not.

\subsection{Reading the measurement map}

Use the table for lookup. Use the notes below when a metric is new, or when you
need the decision the measurement unlocks.

\paragraph{OMA / TDECQ.}
TDECQ asks how much worse this transmitter is than an ideal source after a
reference equalizer. Outer OMA is the optical swing the receiver actually uses.
Together they answer whether the Tx path still has signal-quality margin.
\textbf{Exit when} TDECQ and OMA meet the PMD/ATP at the named pattern and
temperature. \textbf{Decision:} continue, retune bias/equalization, or reject
the transmitter path. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} you chase receiver noise while
the eye was already out of budget.

\paragraph{Extinction ratio / RLM.}
Extinction ratio and level separation mismatch (RLM) set how much OMA you get
at fixed average power and how linear the PAM4 levels are. Poor RLM inflates
TDECQ even when average power looks fine. \textbf{Exit when} ER/RLM meet the
mask at the failing corner. \textbf{Decision:} retune modulator bias or
driver, or accept a derate. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} average-power APC hides
a collapsing outer eye.

\paragraph{Wavelength / SMSR.}
Wavelength placement and side-mode suppression ask whether the spectral ledger
still closes: on-grid for filters or rings, single-mode under temperature and
age. \textbf{Exit when} the line sits in the allowed window with SMSR in
spec. \textbf{Decision:} retune lock/thermal control, derate temperature, or
replace the laser. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} BER failures get blamed on RIN
when the line walked onto a filter edge.

\paragraph{RIN.}
Relative intensity noise sets how far $Q$ can rise with power. Measure with a
quiet bias path and under controlled ORL so you separate intrinsic laser noise
from feedback. \textbf{Exit when} RIN at the stated ORL meets the budget.
\textbf{Decision:} fix reflections/supply, replace the laser, or stop raising
launch into a floor. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} you keep adding photons to a
non-power-limited impairment (\Cref{sec:interview-waterfall}).

\paragraph{Insertion loss / ORL.}
Insertion loss is the first power-ledger line. ORL asks whether reflections
are seeding RIN or bursts. \textbf{Exit when} loss and ORL are inside the
plant assumptions used in the link budget. \textbf{Decision:} clean/replace
connectors, add isolation, or reopen the budget. \textbf{Risk if skipped:}
burst tickets look like random laser death.

\paragraph{Receiver sensitivity.}
Sensitivity is the minimum OMA for the target BER, the budget's bottom line.
A parallel waterfall shift with no floor usually means the Rx path or channel
loss changed. \textbf{Exit when} sensitivity meets the ledger with stated
pattern and stress. \textbf{Decision:} golden-swap ownership, derate reach, or
redesign Rx. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} Tx FA on an Rx-limited link.

\paragraph{Pre-FEC BER / FEC histogram.}
Pre-FEC BER is the system score every other metric feeds. The FEC histogram
shape separates sparse Gaussian-like errors from clustered bursts (MPI,
intermittents, unlocked intervals). \textbf{Exit when} BER and histogram
support the claimed mechanism class. \textbf{Decision:} contain, clean,
retune, or open FA. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} average BER hides a bursty
escape that ATP never stressed.

\paragraph{CMIS state / DDM.}
Management state and digital diagnostics confirm the control and monitor path
before you blame photons. Disagreement between digital diagnostics monitoring
(DDM) and an external meter is itself a finding (monitor-PD or calibration).
\textbf{Exit when} state progression and DDM match bench truth at the named
plane. \textbf{Decision:} fix firmware/seat/power, or proceed to optics.
\textbf{Risk if skipped:} weeks of optical FA on a module that never reached
ready.

\subsection{Why this map is ordered by isolation}

Transmitter metrics come before channel and receiver metrics because a bad Tx
eye contaminates every downstream number. Channel loss and ORL come before Rx
sensitivity arguments for the same reason. Link-level BER is last: it is the
verdict, not the first bisect. If you start at BER alone, you still need this
map to choose the next instrument.

\section{Transmitter and dispersion eye closure quaternary (TDECQ)}
\label{sec:tdecq}

\term{TDECQ} (transmitter and dispersion eye closure quaternary) deserves a closer
look because it is the metric that governs PAM4 transmitter acceptance. It answers
a specific question: \emph{how much worse is this transmitter than an ideal one,
after a realistic receiver has done what it can to clean up the signal?}

\subsection{How it is measured}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Capture.} The optical waveform is acquired on a sampling
      oscilloscope (a DCA) through a standardized reference receiver (a
      fourth-order Bessel--Thomson filter at roughly half the baud rate) so every
      lab measures the same bandwidth.
\item \textbf{Equalize.} A defined \term{reference equalizer}, a
      \term{feed-forward equalizer}\firstterm{FFE} (FFE) with a small, bounded number of taps (commonly
      up to five), is applied. This models the modest equalization a real receiver
      would perform, so the transmitter is not penalized for \term{ISI}\firstterm{ISI} the system can
      remove anyway.
\item \textbf{Histogram.} Two narrow vertical histogram windows are placed inside
      the symbol (near 0.45 and 0.55 of the unit interval). The noise distribution
      is evaluated at the three PAM4 decision thresholds.
\item \textbf{Compute.} The algorithm finds the RMS Gaussian noise
      $\sigma$ that, added to the equalized signal, would just reach a target
      symbol error ratio of $4.8\times10^{-4}$ (the SER consistent with the KP4
      pre-FEC budget). TDECQ is the ratio, in dB, of the noise an \emph{ideal}
      transmitter could tolerate to the noise \emph{this} transmitter can
      tolerate:
      \[
        \mathrm{TDECQ} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{ideal}}}
        {\sigma_{\text{measured}}}\right).
      \]
\end{enumerate}

A worse transmitter tolerates less added noise before failing, so
$\sigma_{\text{measured}}$ shrinks and TDECQ rises. Lower is better; typical
100--200G/lane specifications cap it in the low single-digit dB range.

\subsection{Related quantities and failure signatures}

\begin{description}
\item[SECQ] \firstterm{SECQ} the stressed-eye counterpart used on the receiver side, adding a
      calibrated stressor to test margin rather than transmitter quality alone.
      See \Cref{sec:secq}.
\item[RLM (relative level mismatch)] measures how evenly the four PAM4 levels are
      spaced; poor RLM (uneven levels) inflates TDECQ.
\end{description}

Because TDECQ folds several impairments into one number, the way it fails is
diagnostic: uneven levels point to modulator or driver linearity (RLM);
residual eye closure the equalizer cannot fix points to excess ISI or limited
bandwidth; a noise-limited result points to low OMA, RIN, or reflections. For
external MZMs (TFLN or silicon), also check EO $S_{21}$ bandwidth, $V_\pi$ and
bias quadrature drift with temperature, and RF return loss on the driver-to-modulator
path (\Cref{sec:simzm,sec:tfln-mzm,sec:siring}). This is
why \term{LPO}, which removes the module's own DSP, raises the stakes on
transmitter quality: there is less downstream equalization to hide behind, so
TDECQ-class metrics become even more central.

\section{SECQ and stressed-receiver testing}
\label{sec:secq}

\term{SECQ} (stressed eye closure quaternary) mirrors TDECQ on the \emph{receiver}:
instead of scoring transmitter quality with a reference equalizer, the test applies
a calibrated optical stressor (attenuation, ISI template, optional RIN) and asks
how much margin remains before the receiver hits the target pre-FEC BER.

Stressed-receiver sensitivity and overload tests (\Cref{sec:sensitivity}) use the
same philosophy: bracket the operating OMA range with impairments the link will see
in the field. For LPO, where the module DSP is gone, SECQ-style margin on the
host-side receiver (\Cref{sec:equalization,sec:conditioning}) is as important as
TDECQ on the transmitter.

\section{Instruments}
\label{sec:instruments}

A failing PAM4 link rarely announces which block is wrong. The bench is how you
force the answer: each instrument isolates one failure mode, and the loopback
topology tells you which side of the optical connector owns it.

\begin{description}
\item[DCA] \firstterm{DCA} (digital communication analyzer): sampling scope for PAM4 eyes, TDECQ,
      OMA, RLM (\Cref{sec:tdecq}). Needs a reference receiver filter matched to the
      PHY under test.
\item[BERT]\firstterm{BERT} bit-error ratio at pre- and post-FEC; FEC symbol histograms
      (\Cref{sec:kp4}).
\item[OSA]\firstterm{OSA} wavelength, SMSR, side modes, RIN estimates (\Cref{ch:lasers,sec:rin-values}).
\item[VOA / stressor assembly]\firstterm{VOA} calibrated attenuation and optional ISI for SECQ and
      sensitivity sweeps.
\item[Power meter] average power; pair with DCA for OMA.
\item[Thermal chamber + TEC controller] corner validation; essential for rings
      (\Cref{sec:siring,ch:wdm}) and laser grids.
\end{description}

Use electrical loopback (host SerDes), optical loopback (Tx$\to$Rx on module), and
golden-host/golden-module interop to bisect faults (\Cref{sec:optical-channel}).
If the fault follows the module under golden-host swap, stop blaming the SerDes;
if it stays with the host, stop opening laser FA.

\section{Building a link budget}
\label{sec:link-budget}

A link budget is a signed dB (or power) ledger from transmitter to receiver.
For IM/DD short reach, start from outer OMA at the Tx faceplate and subtract every
loss and penalty until you compare against receiver sensitivity (with target BER
and KP4 pre-FEC threshold, \Cref{sec:kp4,sec:sensitivity}).

\begin{dectree}
Transmitter output (OMA)
  |
Coupling loss
  |
Connector loss
  |
Fiber / waveguide loss
  |
Additional penalties (TDECQ / dispersion / ORL)
  |
Receiver input
  |
Sensitivity requirement
  |
Remaining margin
\end{dectree}

Keep power budget, signal-quality penalties, timing, thermal, and control
authority as separate ledgers when the impairment is not a pure optical-power
number (\Cref{sec:laser-margin-erosion,sec:tree-margin-budget}).

\paragraph{Design allocation versus validation measurement.}
Distinguish margin allocation in design from margin verification in test.
During design, engineers allocate transmitter output, receiver sensitivity,
insertion loss, temperature degradation, aging, jitter, and manufacturing
variation. During customer or system qualification, the integrator often
validates the net behavior across the operating envelope.

\begin{dectree}
Design: allocate line items
  |
Build / integrate
  |
Test: measure net at named plane
  |
Room-T sensitivity margin
  |
Temperature / stress sweep
  |
Observed margin loss
  |
Remaining headroom
  |
Deployment decision
\end{dectree}

\paragraph{Typical ledger (single-mode DR class).}
Start from Tx OMA on the DCA (or from average power and ER). Subtract
connector/coupling loss (1--3~dB per mated pair; fiber attach in CPO), fiber loss
($\sim$0.3--0.4~dB/km at 1310~nm; often negligible at 500~m), and MUX/de-MUX if
WDM (2--5~dB per stage, \Cref{sec:wdm-hardware}). Add penalties for TDECQ (already
in the OMA spec for many PMDs), dispersion (\Cref{sec:chirp-dispersion}), and
ORL/RIN reflection (\Cref{sec:optical-channel,sec:rin-values}). Compare the
remainder to stressed sensitivity at pre-FEC BER $2.4\times10^{-4}$, and keep
1--3~dB+ of production margin (more for fleet corners). Numbers here are
examples for a DR-class sketch, not universal limits.
Electrical budgets parallel this for the host-to-module path: COM and pre-FEC BER
(\Cref{sec:com,sec:eye-budget,sec:equalization}). LPO requires \emph{both} ledgers
to close without module DSP help.

\section{Module management: CMIS}
\label{sec:cmis}

\subsection{What CMIS is, and why an optical engineer cares}

\term{CMIS}\firstterm{CMIS} (Common Management Interface Specification) is the
vendor-neutral management layer between a host (switch ASIC, NIC, or test fixture)
and a pluggable or on-board optical module. The host talks to the module over a
two-wire bus (TWI, I2C-like) through a paged register map: identity, power mode,
alarms, per-lane monitors, and (at 224G/448G) link-training and host signal-
integrity tuning extensions~\cite{cmis53}. CMIS covers QSFP-DD, OSFP, COBO, ELSFP,
and CPO engines that expose the same management contract.

You touch CMIS on every bring-up and every field triage. It is how the host learns
what module is seated, when lasers may turn on, what Tx/Rx power and temperature
look like, and whether a link failed at the management layer or the optical layer.
A module that passes BER on a bench with lasers forced on but cannot reach
ModuleReady on a production host will fail in the fleet (\Cref{sec:bringup}).

\subsection{The module state machine}

CMIS defines a module state machine the host drives. After presence detect and
power application, the module stays in low power until the host releases
\texttt{LPModeL} (or the CMIS 5.x \texttt{LowPwr} equivalent). The host reads
identifier pages, clears sticky interrupts, and steps the module toward
ModuleReady. Only then should Tx lanes or ELS lasers enable. ELSFP modules that
emit before ModuleReady are a reject: the host did not authorize light
(\Cref{sec:elsfp}).

Data paths have their own state machines in CMIS 5.x (data path states, and
network path states for media-side links). For bring-up, map the sequence in
\Cref{sec:bringup} onto these transitions: presence and Vcc, CMIS init and
ModuleReady, enable light, optical path check, electrical lock, traffic, snapshot.
Skipping step 2 and jumping to BER is how interop failures hide until production.

\subsection{The memory map: pages, monitors, control}

The lower memory map holds module identity, status, interrupt flags, and alarm
thresholds. Upper pages hold application descriptors, lane controls, tunable-laser
support, versatile diagnostics (VDM), and command-data-block (CDB) firmware
messaging~\cite{cmis53}. Hosts select an application (lane count, host interface,
media type) before bringing up traffic.

\term{DDM}\firstterm{DDM} (digital diagnostic monitoring) is the telemetry layer
you read at scale: per-lane Tx and Rx optical power, laser bias current when
exposed, module temperature, supply voltage, LOS/LOL flags, and alarm/warning
bits. On WDM parts you also get wavelength or channel ID. This is exactly what
\Cref{sec:fleet-triage} reads before anyone reaches for a DCA. On bring-up, dump
the register map you will use in the field and treat that dump as the golden
reference for later RMA comparisons.

\subsection{CMIS as a validation deliverable}

CMIS correctness is part of production readiness, not a firmware afterthought.
ATP should prove the state machine reaches ModuleReady across voltage and thermal
corners; DDM monitors track bench truth (CMIS Tx power versus DCA, module
temperature versus case $T$); alarms fire at the right thresholds; and firmware
revision is ECO-controlled like laser die revision (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec}).
Multi-source interop failures are often CMIS, media-type, or firmware mismatches,
not marginal TDECQ (\Cref{sec:bringup}). At fleet scale the register map is the
only eyes you have on a module in the rack. If CMIS is wrong, triage starts blind.

\section{Module and system bring-up}
\label{sec:bringup}

Characterization proves a sample can meet metrics on a quiet bench. Bring-up
proves a module (then a system) can be powered, managed, and linked the way
production and the fleet will actually run it. Lab-to-production programs fail in
the gap between those two if you only ever test golden hosts, clean fiber, and
room-temperature faceplates.

\paragraph{Module bring-up sequence.}
Run this order on every new module (pluggable, ELSFP, or CPO engine with CMIS).
Do not skip ahead to BER: a link that ``works'' with lasers forced on and CMIS
ignored will fail the first host that enforces the state machine
(\Cref{sec:elsfp}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Presence and power.} Detect module (\texttt{ModPrsL} or equivalent).
      Apply rails in the host power sequence. Confirm Vcc and module temperature
      in CMIS. Stay in low power (\texttt{LPModeL} asserted or ModuleLowPwr) until
      management is sane.
\item \textbf{CMIS init.} Read identifier, vendor, firmware rev, supported media.
      Clear sticky interrupts. Confirm the state machine can reach ModuleReady
      (or the pluggable equivalent) under host command. Dump the register map you
      will use in the field; that dump is your bring-up golden reference.
\item \textbf{Enable light.} Exit low power; enable Tx lanes / ELS lasers only after
      ModuleReady. Confirm Tx optical power and laser bias (if exposed) against the
      power class. Lasers that come up before the host asks are a reject for ELSFP
      (\Cref{sec:elsfp}).
\item \textbf{Optical path.} Mate fiber (clean first). Check Rx power and
      LOS\firstterm{LOS}. Optical loopback first if the host path is unproven.
\item \textbf{Electrical lock.} Bring host SerDes / module CDR. Confirm
      LOL\firstterm{LOL} clear, equalizer taps not pegged (\Cref{sec:equalization}).
      For LPO, this is the host eye and COM path (\Cref{sec:com,sec:drivers}).
\item \textbf{Traffic.} PRBS\firstterm{PRBS} or live FEC traffic. Pre-FEC BER vs.\ KP4 threshold
      (\Cref{sec:kp4}); glance at FEC symbol-error histogram shape.
\item \textbf{Quality snapshot.} On a Tx-capable path: OMA/RLM/TDECQ or module
      diagnostics that proxy them (\Cref{sec:tdecq}). Record CMIS + BER + case $T$
      together so later triage has a baseline (\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reading the production-corner map}
\label{sec:prod-corners-read}

Bench temperature and voltage sweeps answer how the part behaves. The corners
in \Cref{tab:prod-corners} answer whether that behavior survives the rack,
host, plant, and service actions the fleet will actually apply. Run them
before you call DVT or PVT done.

\paragraph{Chassis thermal.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Does the module hold lock, power, and BER when faceplate temperature and TEC
load match a powered sled, not only a quiet chamber setpoint?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
Chamber case-$T$ does not prove airflow, neighbor heat, or faceplate gradient.
After chassis thermal you know whether derate, TEC, or ring unlock risk is
real under load.

\textbf{Activities.}
Install in the target rack or sled. Apply production airflow and power load.
Log case $T$, TEC current, lock, and pre-FEC BER versus the chamber baseline.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} loaded sled thermal either closes with margin or names a
derate / TEC / unlock restriction.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve the thermal envelope, restrict deployment, or redesign cooling /
lock.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
Quiet-chamber passes unlock or floor in the first dense tray.

\paragraph{Host rails live.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Does laser bias and CMIS stay clean when host supplies switch under SerDes
traffic?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
A lab PSU hides switching noise into bias that looks like RIN
(\Cref{sec:laser-drivers}). After live rails you know whether PSRR, ground,
or APC owns the floor.

\textbf{Activities.}
Power bias and CMIS from the target host with traffic on. Compare BER and
bias telemetry to a quiet supply.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} host-rail corners meet BER and telemetry limits, or a
named supply / grounding fix is required.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve host pairing, demand PSRR/ground work, or hold LPO / linear paths
that cannot tolerate the noise.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
You chase optical RIN while the host injects the noise.

\paragraph{Dirty fiber / ORL.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Does the link survive controlled contamination or ORL stress the way field
installs will?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
Pristine MT fiber hides reflection-driven RIN and burst errors. After clean
versus dirty BER you know whether connector, isolator, or feedback limits
the plant.

\textbf{Activities.}
Apply controlled ORL or contamination on MT/FAU. Compare BER, RIN proxies,
and recovery after clean.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} stressed-ORL BER either meets the plant budget or forces
isolator / connector / service rules.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve plant practice, tighten cleaning / mate rules, or require better
isolation.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
Lab heroes fail the first dirty install and look like random laser RMAs.

\paragraph{Cable plant.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Does the link budget survive production fiber length, MPO count, and bend
radius?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
A short golden patchcord does not prove the ledger. After production plant
you know whether extra loss and reflections eat the assumed margin
(\Cref{sec:link-budget}).

\textbf{Activities.}
Run the production cable set. Measure loss, ORL, and BER against the signed
budget.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} production plant closes the budget with remaining margin,
or the ledger is revised.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve plant design, cut reach or connectors, or raise launch / Rx
requirements.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
Budget fiction becomes field brownouts on long MPO chains.

\paragraph{ELS hot-swap.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Can the external laser source be pulled and replaced under the service model
the architecture promised (\Cref{sec:elsfp})?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
Static bring-up does not prove CMIS state machines, mate wear, or traffic
recovery under swap. After hot-swap (or controlled stop per CMIS) you know
whether service is real.

\textbf{Activities.}
Pull/replace ELSFP under traffic or under the documented traffic stop. Log
state machine, mate cycles, and recovery BER.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} swap recovers to ready state and BER without undefined
hangs, or service is restricted.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve field-replaceable ELS service, or forbid hot-swap and rewrite ops.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
The replaceable-laser story fails the first maintenance window.

\paragraph{Neighbor load.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Do crosstalk, shared supply droop, and thermal crosstalk stay inside margin
when adjacent modules and lanes run full traffic at max case $T$?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
A lone hero module hides tray-level coupling. After neighbor load you know
whether WDM lock, SI, or PSU owns the fail.

\textbf{Activities.}
Light neighbors at full traffic and temperature. Watch lock, BER, supply
droop, and lane coupling.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} loaded-neighbor corners close, or a spacing / power /
lock restriction is named.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve dense packing, derate neighbor density, or redesign supply / lock.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
Single-module DVT passes; tray bring-up fails.

\paragraph{LPO / linear path.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Does host channel operating margin (COM) and pre-FEC BER close without a
module DSP crutch (\Cref{sec:224g-deploy,sec:com,sec:drivers})?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
Retimed modules hide host FIR and module linearity faults. After the linear
path corner you know whether LPO is shippable on the target host.

\textbf{Activities.}
Run host COM and pre-FEC BER on the linear electrical path. Sweep host
equalization as needed.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} LPO/linear BER and COM meet targets on the production
host, or LPO is rejected for that host class.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve LPO deployment, force retimed optics, or redesign host FIR / module
linearity.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
LPO ships on hope and fails first on the production ASIC SerDes.

\paragraph{Voltage corners.}

\textbf{Purpose.}
Do brown-out and CMIS glitches stay clear at host Vcc min/max under traffic?

\textbf{Uncertainty removed.}
Nominal Vcc hides state-machine and bias glitches at rail edges. After
voltage corners you know whether power design and ATP cover the envelope.

\textbf{Activities.}
Sweep host Vcc with traffic. Log CMIS state, alarms, and BER.

\textbf{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} min/max Vcc corners pass with stable management and BER,
or power design changes.

\textbf{Decision unlocked.}
Approve voltage envelope, tighten ATP voltage screens, or redesign power.

\textbf{Risk if skipped.}
Field brown-outs look like firmware or optics bugs.

\subsection{Why these corners come after quiet characterization}

Quiet $T$/$V$ characterization maps the part. Production corners ask whether
that map survives chassis heat, live host noise, dirty plant, service swaps,
neighbors, linear hosts, and rail edges. Later fleet monitoring must not be
asked to invent coverage that these corners never ran. Chassis thermal, host
rails, and ORL are the minimum before you call bring-up or DVT done on a
representative unit; the full set in \Cref{tab:prod-corners} belongs before
PVT exit.

\subsection{Learning summary}

\begin{description}
\item[Chassis thermal] Does sled airflow and faceplate $T$ match the claim?
\item[Host rails / voltage] Does live supply noise and rail edge stay clean?
\item[Dirty fiber / cable plant] Does the real plant leave margin?
\item[ELS hot-swap / neighbors] Does service and dense packing survive?
\item[LPO / linear path] Does the host close without module DSP?
\end{description}

\paragraph{System bring-up.}
A module that passes on a golden host can still fail in a real chassis:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Host path:} run the same sequence on the target NIC/switch ASIC SerDes,
      not only the lab BERT. LPO and half-retimed modules expose host FIR/CTLE
      mistakes that a retimed module hid (\Cref{sec:conditioning,sec:pluggables}).
\item \textbf{Multi-lane / multi-module:} bring all lanes on a port, then neighbors
      in the same cage or tray. Watch thermal rise, supply droop, and CMIS temp
      alarms when the tray is loaded.
\item \textbf{Golden swap:} known-good module in the suspect host slot, then suspect
      module in a known-good slot. That single swap splits host vs.\ module before
      you open FA (\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).
\item \textbf{Interop:} at least one other vendor host or module if the program
      claims multi-source. Interop failures are usually CMIS, media type, or
      electrical eye, not laser physics.
\item \textbf{ELS / CPO:} external laser modules add a second bring-up: ELSFP state
      machine and optical mate to the engine, then engine bring-up with light
      present (\Cref{sec:elsfp,sec:cpo-status}). A dark engine with a healthy ELS is
      an optical connector or FAU problem until proven otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Exit criteria before ``bring-up done.''}
Call module bring-up done only when: CMIS state machine and enable sequence are
correct; pre-FEC BER meets target on the \emph{target} host with margin; a CMIS+BER+$T$
snapshot is filed; and at least the chassis-thermal, host-rails, and ORL corners
in \Cref{tab:prod-corners} have been run on a representative unit. Call system
bring-up done when golden-swap has split host vs.\ module issues and multi-lane /
neighbor load has not opened a new failure mode. Everything after that is
characterization depth, supplier gates (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec}), or fleet triage
(\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).

\keyidea{Bring-up is a sequence (presence $\to$ CMIS $\to$ light $\to$ lock $\to$
BER $\to$ snapshot), then a system proof on the real host, then
production-representative corners (chassis thermal, host-rail noise, ORL, ELS
hot-swap, neighbor load). A quiet bench pass is not DVT.}

\section{The debug mindset}
\label{sec:debug}

Debug at this level is data-driven, not opinion-driven. The method is disciplined
bisection: change one domain at a time, and let the measurement tell you whether
the transmitter, the channel, or the receiver moved.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Isolate transmitter versus channel versus receiver, using loopbacks.
\item Sweep temperature and voltage to expose corner-dependent failures.
\item Correlate failures to DSP equalizer tap values (\Cref{sec:equalization}) and FEC symbol-error
      statistics (\Cref{sec:kp4}); these tell you \emph{how} the link fails.
\end{enumerate}

The third step is where modern PAM4 links differ from older eye-mask work. Tap
saturation and FEC histograms often reveal the failure mode before a single
waveform screenshot does. Treat those as primary evidence, not as afterthoughts
logged once BER already fails.

\aside{Worked example. ``The link fails only at high temperature.'' Candidate
causes: laser wavelength drift off the grid or off a ring resonance; TEC
saturation; EAM bias shift; elevated receiver thermal noise; LIV rollover
(\Cref{sec:laser-aging,sec:laser-params}). You bisect with
measurements (spectrum for wavelength, LIV for the laser, receiver sensitivity
at temperature) rather than guessing.}

\section{The debugging fork in validation}
\label{sec:validation-fork}

Apply the debugging fork (\Cref{sec:debug-fork}) before sweeping parameters or
changing firmware: check the power meter or CMIS Rx power monitor first. If power
moved, the fault is in the optical path (laser, coupling, connector, fiber, MUX);
if power held but BER or TDECQ worsened, it is signal quality (bandwidth, noise,
jitter, bias, equalization, reflection). This one check prevents the most common
validation mistake: retuning an equalizer or laser bias when the real cause is a
dirty connector. Then check which margin ledger moved

\section{Fleet and field triage}
\label{sec:fleet-triage}

Lab debug asks: \emph{what is broken on this unit?} Fleet triage asks:
\emph{which bucket does this failure belong in, and who owns the fix?} Optical
programs at fleet scale own that split across performance, reliability, and
manufacturability. Wrong bucket wastes weeks (sending a contaminated connector to
laser FA, or rewriting a SerDes FIR when the laser is rolling over).

\paragraph{Three buckets.}
Classify every field issue before deep root-cause work:

\begin{description}
\item[Performance] the design or operating point does not close the budget under
      the conditions seen in the fleet. Examples: TDECQ/RLM marginal at case
      temperature, host COM tight on LPO, ring unlock under thermal crosstalk,
      ORL-driven RIN that the architecture assumed away. Fix is usually retune,
      derate, firmware, or a design/spec change (\Cref{sec:tdecq,sec:com,sec:siring}).
\item[Reliability] the unit met spec at ship and later degraded. Examples: LIV
      threshold rise, SMSR collapse, EAM bias creep, COD, TEC wear, epoxy creep on
      fiber attach. Fix is Arrhenius-backed life projection, burn-in/screen, derating,
      or field-replaceable lasers
      (\Cref{sec:wearout-modes,sec:laser-aging,sec:gr468,sec:elsfp}).
\item[Manufacturability] a subpopulation fails early or never met the ATP; the
      issue tracks lot, date code, supplier site, or assembly step. Examples: FAU
      misalign yield cliff, solder void on a driver die attach, incoming DPPM spike,
      CMIS register map mismatch on one firmware rev. Fix is SPC, ATP tighten,
      first-article, DPA, and 8D/CAPA with the supplier
      (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec,sec:photonic-packaging}).
\end{description}

A single symptom can sit in more than one bucket until you bisect. The tree below
forces the split with telemetry first, then a short bench confirm, then an RMA
label. \Cref{ch:failure-modes} expands the same method into symptom-led bench and
fleet procedures.

\paragraph{Telemetry you actually read.}
At scale you rarely start with a DCA. Start with what the host and module already
report:

\begin{itemize}
\item \term{CMIS} monitors and alarms: module temperature, supply
      rails, Tx/Rx optical power, laser bias (when exposed), wavelength or channel
      ID on WDM parts, LOS/LOL flags, and interrupt history (\texttt{IntL} on ELSFP;
      \Cref{sec:elsfp}).
\item Host link state: CDR lock, pre-FEC BER, FEC symbol-error histogram shape
      (\Cref{sec:kp4}), equalizer tap saturation (\Cref{sec:equalization}).
\item Fleet context: rack position, case temperature, time since install, date
      code / lot, neighbor-link correlation (one bad fiber vs whole tray).
\end{itemize}

\Cref{tab:fleet-triage} is the working map. Read left to right: observe, check
telemetry, pick a provisional bucket, then run the named confirm measurement
before you open an RMA or change a design rule.

\subsection{Reading the fleet triage map}

Each row is a provisional route, not a confirmed root cause. Capture telemetry
first. Confirm with the smallest measurement that can falsify the bucket. Then
assign an owner.

\paragraph{Link never comes up (fresh install).}
Ask whether the part is seated, powered, and managed before you open laser FA.
CMIS presence, supply rails, Tx power flatline, and loss-of-signal (LOS) split
install from product. Confirm with visual fiber/connector checks, a golden
module swap, and a frozen CMIS dump. \textbf{Decision:} ops fix, or supplier
ATP if the fail tracks a lot. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} manufacturing escapes
get filed as design defects.

\paragraph{Intermittent LOS / burst errors.}
Ask whether the plant is reflecting or contaminating. Rx power dropouts and
bursty FEC histograms point at connectors or ORL before intrinsic RIN. Confirm
with inspect/clean, ORL meter, and RIN versus ORL. \textbf{Decision:} cleaning
discipline or packaging FA if RMAs repeat. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} burst
tickets become endless laser replacements.

\paragraph{Pre-FEC BER high, power OK.}
Power held, so leave the power ledger. Tap saturation, logged TDECQ/RLM, and
case temperature point at signal quality or host SI. Confirm on a DCA and with
host channel operating margin (COM) thinking on linear paths.
\textbf{Decision:} host SI, module Tx design, or retune. \textbf{Risk if
skipped:} you reseat fiber forever on a quality-path fail.

\paragraph{BER rises only at high case $T$.}
Ask whether the operating point or the device changed with heat. Telemetry for
temp alarms, Tx sag, and wavelength walk comes first. Confirm with LIV at
temperature, OSA, TEC/heater codes, and modulator bias. Cool-down recovery
raises $P(\mathrm{operating\ point})$; permanent shift raises
$P(\mathrm{aging})$. \textbf{Decision:} derate, thermal design, or supplier
life action. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} ambient-only debug misses the spent
control ledger.

\paragraph{Slow BER creep over weeks/months.}
Ask whether wear-out is spending the noise or power ledger. Bias current
rising at fixed Tx power is a classic aging tell. Confirm LIV/SMSR against
ship ATP and lot history. \textbf{Decision:} replace, derate, or update burn-
in. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} FIT models stay optimistic until the fleet
teaches you.

\paragraph{Sudden hard fail, was healthy.}
Ask whether the event is catastrophic optical damage, ESD, or a shared
infrastructure hit. Neighbor links and the last good CMIS snapshot matter.
Confirm with dark LIV and physical FA; check date-code clusters.
\textbf{Decision:} FA plus supplier 8D, or infrastructure fix.
\textbf{Risk if skipped:} one COD event becomes a false process CAPA.

\paragraph{One date code / site fails early.}
Ask whether this is a manufacturing subpopulation. Lot Pareto and burn-in
escape rate are the first cuts. Confirm incoming SPC versus ATP and FA on a
sample. \textbf{Decision:} quarantine, CAPA, hold shipment. \textbf{Risk if
skipped:} a bad lot keeps deploying while FA studies one unit.

\paragraph{WDM / ring unlock, power OK.}
Ask whether the spectral or lock ledger was spent while average power looked
fine. Channel ID, neighbor thermal, and lock-loop status are the telemetry.
Confirm resonance tune, crosstalk, and line power. \textbf{Decision:} lock
firmware or thermal design. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} unlocks get mislabeled
as random BER.

\paragraph{ELSFP swap restores link.}
Ask whether the external laser, the connector, or the engine owned the fail.
Compare old versus new CMIS and connector cycles. Confirm MT inspect and LIV
on the returned module. \textbf{Decision:} split RMA codes for laser versus
connector. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} FIT burns down the wrong wear-out mode
(\Cref{sec:elsfp}).

\subsection{Why triage order matters}

Scope before mechanism. Telemetry before destructive FA. Bucket before owner.
Confirm before CAPA. Closing the loop into ATP is part of the incident, not
optional paperwork. Reversing that order produces NFF piles and merged RMA
codes that make life models dishonest.

\paragraph{How to walk an incident (order of operations).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Stabilize and capture.} Freeze CMIS dump, host BER/FEC counters, rack
      $T$, and install age before anyone reseats the module. Reseating destroys
      connector evidence.
\item \textbf{Localize.} One link vs tray vs rack. Tray-wide points at power,
      cooling, or a shared ELS. Single-link points at that module, fiber, or host
      lane.
\item \textbf{Classify} with \Cref{tab:fleet-triage}. Write the bucket on the
      ticket before FA starts.
\item \textbf{Confirm} with the smallest measurement that can falsify the bucket
      (golden swap, clean/inspect, LIV, TDECQ, ORL). Do not skip to DPA.
\item \textbf{Act.}
      \begin{itemize}
      \item Performance: change operating policy (derate, FIR, lock loop) or open a
            design/spec defect.
      \item Reliability: replace (ELSFP hot-swap when available), update FIT burn-down,
            tighten burn-in or derate (\Cref{sec:laser-aging,sec:fit-example}).
      \item Manufacturability: quarantine lot, incoming hold, supplier 8D with DPA
            photos and ATP deltas (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec}).
      \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Close the loop.} Feed the signature back into ATP and CMIS alarm
      thresholds so the next incident trips earlier.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Worked paths (three common tickets).}

\emph{``High temp only.''}
CMIS shows module near thermal limit and Tx power sagging. Bucket starts as
performance (thermal design / derate) until LIV at temperature shows threshold
rise matching an aged lot, which flips it to reliability. Measure OSA wavelength
before blaming the laser: a ring unlock is still performance
(\Cref{sec:siring,ch:wdm}).

\emph{``Random burst errors, average power fine.''}
Check FEC histogram for clustered errors and CMIS for Rx power dropouts. Clean
and measure ORL. If RIN rises with ORL, it is performance/architecture
(feedback). If ORL is fine and bursts track a date code, it is mfg (intermittent
fiber attach). If bursts grow over months at fixed ORL, suspect laser or driver
aging (\Cref{sec:laser-drivers,sec:laser-aging}).

\emph{``ELSFP replace fixed it; returned module looks alive on the bench.''}
Alive LIV with high ORL sensitivity or dirty MT face means connector/ORL
(mfg/ops), not laser death. Dead or kinked LIV means reliability. Split those RMA
codes explicitly or your FIT math will blame the wrong wear-out mode
(\Cref{sec:elsfp,sec:photonic-packaging}).

\paragraph{RMA labels that keep FIT honest.}
RMA\firstterm{RMA} codes should be distinct, not a single ``optics fail'':
\begin{itemize}
\item laser wear-out (LIV/SMSR/EAM aging confirmed);
\item COD / sudden dark;
\item connector / contamination / ORL;
\item fiber attach / FAU;
\item driver / bias electronics;
\item host / SerDes / LPO eye (not module);
\item NFF\firstterm{NFF} (no-fault-found; track these; high NFF means bad triage).
\end{itemize}
NFF rate and lot Pareto are as important as FIT. A rising NFF with clean LIV
points at install practice or intermittent connectors, not Arrhenius.

\section{Engineering lens}
\label{sec:validation-engineering-lens}

\subsection{How it works}

Validation is a chain of evidence, not a single pass: a number means nothing
without its reference plane, its corner, and its method. The chapter's ladder,
instruments, and triage tree are that evidence chain from bench to fleet.

\subsection{How it is measured}
\label{sec:validation-how-measured}

Use the least complex instrument that can falsify the current hypothesis.
\Cref{tab:measurement-mapping} maps every key metric to its instrument, rationale,
and failure signature in one lookup; the bring-up sequence
(\Cref{sec:bringup,sec:prod-corners}) orders those instruments into a workflow.

\subsection{How it fails}
\label{sec:validation-how-fails}

Validation fails when the setup, sample, or acceptance rule does not match the
product. Common misses are a stale calibration, the wrong reference plane, a
golden host that hides interop risk, pristine fiber that hides ORL sensitivity,
short BER dwell, one lane tested without neighbors, and chamber temperature used
as a substitute for measured case temperature. These are test escapes even when
the device physics is sound.

\failuremode{Low optical power}{A lane is dark or below its launch-power limit.}{A
laser or enable fault, coupling loss, connector contamination, fiber polarity,
calibration error, or a power-meter setup mistake.}{Known source and meter,
inspection scope, CMIS state and bias, power at successive planes, and a golden
fiber or module.}{Correct the setup first, then repair the failing source, attach,
connector, or control path. Add the signature at the earliest production test
that can catch it.}

\subsection{How it is debugged}
\label{sec:validation-how-debugged}

Preserve the failing state and record software, firmware, calibration, fixture,
cables, temperature, and supply. Verify the meter with a known source. Walk from
power to spectrum to waveform to BER, moving one reference plane at a time. Use a
golden swap to split host, module, and fiber. Only then stress temperature, voltage,
ORL, and neighbors. Every corrective action needs a repeated failing test, a
repeated passing test, and a guard against recurrence in ATP or telemetry.

\debugstory{A new module lot showed low optical power on one station.}{The same
units passed on a second station. A known source exposed an offset in the first
power-meter path.}{The lot was good, and the station was reading low.}{A reference
jumper had been replaced without updating the path-loss calibration.}{The station
was recalibrated, jumper identity was placed under change control, and a start-of-
shift source check was added.}

\section{Interview and design review questions}
\label{sec:validation-design-review}

\paragraph{Concept.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Why is a passing BER on a golden bench not sufficient for production
      readiness?
\item What is the difference between characterization and validation?
\item Why does LPO raise the stakes on transmitter TDECQ?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Design.}
\begin{itemize}
\item What requirement does each test prove, at which plane, and with which
      uncertainty and guardband?
\item Which corner is represented only by a lab fixture rather than the target
      host or chassis?
\item What is the fastest measurement that can falsify each top risk?
\item Which setup error can make a bad unit pass or a good unit fail?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Debug.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A new module passes on the bench but fails on the production host. What is
      your first measurement?
\item BER is high but optical power looks fine. Apply the debugging fork
      (\Cref{sec:validation-fork}): what do you check next?
\item A module works on host A but fails on host B. How do you determine
      ownership?
\item Are raw data, calibration state, firmware, and sample identity stored well
      enough to replay a failure months later?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Manufacturing and operations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item What is the minimum set of corners that proves production readiness?
\item How do you detect tester drift before it becomes a yield cliff or a field
      escape?
\item What exit criteria distinguish DVT from PVT?
\end{itemize}

\keyidea{Validation is a chain of evidence. Start with calibrated power and
management state, move through spectrum and waveform, then trust BER only after
the blocks and reference planes are known. Run the target host, chassis, fiber,
and neighbor corners before calling the product ready.}
