\chapter{Optical validation}
\label{ch:validation}

A datasheet that closes on a quiet bench is not a product. \emph{Validation}
reduces uncertainty about whether a link meets its requirements across the
temperatures, hosts, connectors, production spread, and lifetime the fleet will
actually see. Passing tests is an output, not the purpose. This chapter walks the
ladder from a single device to a deployed fleet, the engineering question at
each stage, module and system bring-up under production-like corners, and the
hypothesis-driven debug method the work demands.

Debugging asks which margin ledger was exhausted. Qualification asks how much
margin remains after the expected stresses. Both are uncertainty reduction that
ends in a decision (\Cref{app:decision-trees,sec:tree-evidence-block}).

\paragraph{Operating definitions used in this book.}
Companies may use EVT, DVT, PVT, verification, and qualification differently.
Unless noted otherwise, this book uses the questions below. Abbreviations are
collected in \Cref{app:abbreviations}.

\begin{description}
\item[Characterization]
      \emph{Question:} How does the design behave?\\
      \emph{Purpose:} Discover behavior, measure distributions, find cliffs, and
      understand sensitivity. Exploratory: a characterization failure improves
      understanding; it does not automatically fail the product.\\
      \emph{Examples:} BER versus temperature, wavelength drift, receiver
      sensitivity, RIN behavior, bias trends.
\item[Verification]
      \emph{Question:} Does this implementation meet a stated requirement?\\
      \emph{Purpose:} Requirement-based confirmation with a named method and
      reference plane.\\
      \emph{Examples:} transmit power, BER, wavelength accuracy, thermal
      operating range.
\item[Validation]
      \emph{Question:} Does the product work for the intended customer and system
      use?\\
      \emph{Purpose:} System-context proof across hosts, cables, temperatures,
      workloads, and deployment practice.\\
      \emph{Examples:} supported switches, cable plant, expected temperatures,
      real traffic mix.
\item[Qualification]
      \emph{Question:} Do we have evidence this design survives expected variation
      and life?\\
      \emph{Purpose:} Confidence building across environmental stress,
      reliability, manufacturing variation, and supplier variation.\\
      \emph{Examples:} HTOL with a named mechanism, temperature cycling, humidity,
      multi-lot process corners.
\item[Production test]
      \emph{Question:} Can we repeatedly detect unacceptable units?\\
      \emph{Purpose:} Factory and incoming screens that catch escapes at volume.
      Production testing is not qualification.\\
      \emph{Examples:} ATP, lot sampling, SPC, periodic audits.
\item[ATP (acceptance test)]
      A replayable production decision process applied per unit, per lot, or
      according to a documented sampling plan. One form of production test.
\end{description}

\section{The validation ladder}
\label{sec:validation-workflow}

Optical programs fail in the same places again and again: a part that looks good
in characterization but cannot bring up on a production host, or a module that
passes acceptance test plan (\term{ATP}\firstterm{ATP}) and then unlocks
under neighbor heat. The ladder below is a decision framework, not a test menu.
Each stage answers one question the previous stage could not answer. Skipping a
rung does not save time. It moves the escape into a later, more expensive stage.

The core principle is uncertainty reduction. At every stage ask: what do we know
before this stage, what uncertainty remains, what evidence removes it, what
decision that evidence unlocks, and what risk remains afterward
(\Cref{sec:tree-evidence-block}).

\subsection{Requirements definition}
\label{sec:ladder-requirements}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Prevent validating the wrong product. The first validation step is not a
measurement. It is defining what success means.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Without a requirements slice you do not know which BER, envelope, lifetime,
volume, or deployment environment later measurements must close.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Workload and reach intent; host and cable plant constraints; power and thermal
envelope; reliability and service model; manufacturing volume and supplier
capability; telemetry and field-replaceability expectations
(\Cref{sec:systems-engineering-loop,tab:laser-prd}).

\paragraph{Measurements.}
None yet. Write the requirement classes:

\begin{description}
\item[Performance] Data rate, BER/FEC target, latency, power, reach.
\item[Environment] Temperature, humidity, vibration, contamination.
\item[Reliability] Lifetime, failure-rate target, service life.
\item[Manufacturing] Yield intent, test time, supplier capability.
\item[Operational] Telemetry, diagnostics, field replacement.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Required access level.}
System and product owners. No hardware access required.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
A signed requirements slice with owners, planes where applicable, and
pass/fail language that later stages can fail clearly.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Map each requirement to the risk that would falsify it. Those risks become
validation stages.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} the requirements slice is specific enough that architecture
and later stages have pass criteria, or when you refuse to start hardware work
until it is.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Architecture target and validation scope, or hold until success is defined.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Weak interview answer: ``We test BER.'' Strong answer: first define target BER,
operating envelope, lifetime, manufacturing volume, and deployment environment;
then design validation around the risks.

\subsection{Architecture review}
\label{sec:ladder-architecture}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Determine whether the architecture can meet the requirements before hardware
exists. Validation starts on paper and in models
(\Cref{sec:systems-engineering-loop,tab:laser-engineering-checklist}).

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Even with clear requirements, you may not know whether optical power, thermal
budget, electrical margin, reliability target, and manufacturing cost can close
together.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Link budget, thermal model, power model, noise model, reliability assumptions,
and the manufacturing and service model implied by the requirements slice.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
Simulations, budget closures, and trade studies. Prototype hardware is optional
here; feasibility is the question.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Design and systems owners. Engineering samples are not required for the gate
itself.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Closed or explicitly open budget lines; named assumptions; redesign triggers.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Ask whether the architecture can close optical power, thermal budget,
electrical margin, reliability target, and manufacturing cost without heroic
assumptions.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} the architecture is feasible under stated assumptions, or
when you redesign before buying tooling.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Proceed to hardware bring-up, or redesign.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Treating a quiet-bench prototype as architecture proof. Skipping reliability
and manufacturing cost until DVT.

\subsection{Engineering bring-up}
\label{sec:ladder-bringup}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Answer: is this hardware alive and measurable? Bring-up is not qualification.
Separate questions that programs often collapse:

\begin{description}
\item[Bench bring-up] Does the unit operate on a trusted setup well enough to
      produce interpretable measurements?
\item[System integration bring-up] Does it initialize and carry traffic on the
      target host?
\item[Margin / interop] Does it retain headroom across loaded corners? That is
      later ladder exit evidence, not bring-up exit evidence.
\end{description}

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Before bring-up you do not know whether a failure is integration (seat, cable,
firmware, host) or product physics.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Trusted setup or target host; known-good fiber; firmware load; CMIS tool chain
(\Cref{sec:bringup,tab:bringup-checklist}).

\paragraph{Measurements.}
\begin{description}
\item[Electrical] Rails, clocks, initialization, host link.
\item[Optical] Tx power, Rx power, wavelength, basic BER.
\item[Control] Firmware, CMIS state, alarms.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Black-box host and management access first. Engineering access only when basic
operation cannot be established (\Cref{sec:tree-blackbox-access}).

\paragraph{Data collected.}
CMIS state progression, first-light and Rx power, CDR lock, usable pre-FEC BER
at a named plane, snapshot for later RMA.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Confirm the unit can emit, receive, and pass data under controlled conditions
before arguing about margin or life.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} the unit reaches ready state, emits and receives light in
class, holds CDR lock, and shows a usable pre-FEC BER on the golden or target
host with named reference planes.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Continue into characterization, or stop and debug integration.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Deep failure analysis before basic operation. Treating loaded chassis corners
as bring-up exit evidence. Spending weeks optimizing TDECQ on a part that never
reliably links in a real chassis.

\tradeoff{Bring-up speed vs characterization depth}{Faster path into margin work}{Missing
baselines that make later BER tickets uninterpretable}{When the unit already
links and emits in class on the target host}{Do not skip characterization to
``save time.'' Without a baseline, margin and FA burn calendar later.}

\subsection{Characterization}
\label{sec:ladder-characterization}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Create the behavioral model. The output is not pass/fail. The output is: this
is how the system behaves. Characterization is exploratory. Finding a cliff
improves understanding; it does not automatically fail the product.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Before characterization you know one corner works. After it you know how
distributions move with stress variables and which ledgers (power, noise,
timing, spectral, control) are thin.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Bring-up-complete units; temperature and voltage control; ORL or attenuation
fixtures; multi-unit and multi-lot samples when available.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
\begin{description}
\item[Temperature sweep] BER, power, wavelength, bias, current versus $T$.
\item[Voltage sweep] Electrical margin versus supply.
\item[Optical stress] Attenuation, ORL, contamination, connector conditions.
\item[Distributions] Unit-to-unit and lot-to-lot variation; short-term
      stability and preconditioning. Projected aging and permanent degradation
      belong to reliability qualification.
\end{description}
On the transmitter path, measure TDECQ, OMA, ER, and level linearity. With
component access, add LIV, RIN, and SMSR
(\Cref{sec:tdecq,sec:link-budget,sec:laser-params}).

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Black-box metrics first. Engineering access for LIV/RIN/SMSR when the
behavioral model needs physical baselines.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Response surfaces, distribution summaries, thin-ledger list, and candidate
guardbands.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Separate response mapping, distribution estimation, specification verification
against a named method and plane, and mechanism diagnostics. This data later
feeds specifications, guardbands, ATP limits, and reliability plans.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} population behavior versus the required corners is mapped,
thin ledgers are named, and you decide whether the design needs derate or
redesign before loaded-fleet work.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Proceed to margin validation, derate the envelope, or redesign.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Treating a hero sample at 25~$^\circ$C as the fleet model. Confusing
characterization cliffs with automatic product failure. Parking life
projection inside characterization.

\subsection{Margin validation}
\label{sec:ladder-margin}
\label{sec:ladder-margin-interop}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
A product does not fail because it reaches a nominal limit. It fails because
all margins are consumed. Measure remaining headroom under loaded, production-
like stress (\Cref{sec:prod-corners,sec:laser-margin-erosion}).

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Characterization maps the part. Extra uncertainty remains about how much
capability survives temperature, voltage, loss, ORL, aging intent, and
manufacturing variation when they stack.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Characterization baselines; production-representative corners; named reference
planes; the requirements BER/FEC and envelope targets.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
Loaded corners: target sled airflow, host rails with SerDes traffic,
controlled contamination or ORL stress, production fiber length and bend
radius, ELS hot-swap if promised, neighbor modules at full load
(\Cref{tab:prod-corners}). Compare pre-FEC BER, telemetry, retrain count, and
control headroom against the characterization baseline.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Black-box remaining-margin metrics first. External optical eye only when
access exists and the corner needs it (\Cref{sec:tree-blackbox-access}).

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Cliff locations, remaining headroom by ledger, and a margin waterfall that
does not double-count penalties.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Available margin is design capability minus the required operating condition.
Track categories separately, then stack once:

\begin{description}
\item[Optical] Tx power, receiver sensitivity, insertion loss.
\item[Electrical] Eye opening, jitter, equalization reserve.
\item[Thermal] Wavelength drift, laser efficiency, receiver noise.
\item[Reliability] Aging and wear-out allowances (intent from life plan).
\item[Manufacturing] Unit variation and process drift.
\end{description}

\noindent\textit{Margin waterfall (illustrative accounting, not a universal
budget).}
\begin{quote}
Initial optical margin\\
$-$ temperature penalty\\
$-$ connector / plant loss\\
$-$ aging penalty\\
$-$ contamination / ORL penalty\\
$-$ manufacturing variation\\
$=$ remaining field margin
\end{quote}
\tradeoff{More optical margin vs cost and power}{Reach and corner tolerance under
temperature, aging, and plant loss}{Laser power, thermal load, and efficiency
(\Cref{sec:laser-margin-erosion})}{When a named uncertainty still dominates the
remaining risk}{Allocate margin where uncertainty is highest; do not maximize
every ledger equally.}

Do not subtract the same physical effect twice under two names. If ORL stress
is already in the loaded corner, do not also invent a second reflection tax
without new evidence.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Margin exit:} failure cliffs and remaining headroom are known at the
named plane and loaded corners.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Approve the fleet corner envelope, restrict deployment conditions, or send the
design back.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Quiet-bench margin treated as rack margin. Double-counting temperature and
thermal-wavelength penalties. Calling interop tickets ``laser failures'' when
the escape was an untested corner.

\subsection{Interoperability validation}
\label{sec:ladder-interop}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
A component can pass validation and still fail as a system. Prove supported
host, peer, firmware, and channel combinations retain required headroom.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Margin on a golden host does not prove the supported ecosystem.

\tradeoff{Golden-host speed vs interop risk}{Fast bring-up and clean debug on a
known good station}{Hidden host, firmware, or plant sensitivity until volume}{When
golden-host data are a stage gate, not the exit criteria for the ecosystem}{Use
golden hosts for speed; require representative hosts before calling interop done.}

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Margin baselines; claimed host and peer matrix; firmware revisions; cable and
fiber plant classes.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
Exercise the matrix dimensions (Host: switch ASIC / SerDes; Peer: optical
module or second source; Software: firmware and CMIS revision/states;
Environment: faceplate temperature and airflow; Channel: fiber/cable plant
and connectors).

Useful priors for tickets include CMIS state, media type, and electrical eye;
those are starting points, not a general law that the laser path is innocent.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Black-box customer-visible metrics on target hosts. Engineering access only
when ownership cannot be assigned from swaps and telemetry.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Supported combination list, documented restrictions, failing combination
signatures.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Assign failures to host, peer, software, environment, or channel before
changing laser bias tables.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Interop exit:} supported host, peer, firmware, and channel combinations
retain the required headroom, or a documented restriction defines where the
product may ship.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Approve the supported ecosystem, restrict SKUs or hosts, or reopen design.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
One golden host as ``interop done.'' Merging margin cliffs with peer bugs into
one vague fail bucket.

\subsection{Reliability qualification}
\label{sec:ladder-stress}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Build mechanism-based evidence that the design survives expected variation and
life. Never run stresses only because a checklist names them. Keep three jobs
distinct:

\begin{description}
\item[Operational environment test] Does it work while exposed?
\item[Reliability stress] Does exposure cause unacceptable permanent change?
\item[Life projection] What field-life claim is justified, with what sample,
      confidence, and assumptions?
\end{description}

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Functional and margin stages do not answer lifetime
(\Cref{sec:gr468,sec:ic-reliability,sec:tree-qual-evidence}).

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Named failure mechanisms from the requirements and architecture risk list;
sample plan; acceptance criteria; production-proxy intent.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
Follow the evidence path: failure mechanism $\rightarrow$ acceleration method
$\rightarrow$ stress $\rightarrow$ pre/post observable $\rightarrow$
acceptance criterion $\rightarrow$ confidence. Examples by mechanism intent:

\begin{description}
\item[Temperature cycling] Solder fatigue, CTE mismatch, mechanical stress.
\item[Humidity] Corrosion, moisture ingress.
\item[HTOL] Semiconductor degradation under bias and temperature.
\item[Vibration] Mechanical failures.
\end{description}
A stress without a failure mechanism is only exposure. Deep FIT/DPPM math lives
in \Cref{ch:reliability}; this stage owns the logic.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Qual lab access for stressed populations; black-box customer-visible margins
before and after stress; engineering baselines (LIV, SMSR, spectrum) when
returns need physical confirmation.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Pre/post stress margins, mechanism notes, sample and confidence statement,
production-proxy candidates.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Justify $E_a$ and stress relevance for the named mechanism. A life claim
without a mechanism is a narrative, not evidence
(\Cref{sec:tree-qual-evidence,sec:tree-evidence-block}).

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} the sample plan, mechanism, and projected life support the
requirements slice, or when you explicitly hold ship for life risk.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Accept life risk for the envelope, derate life or use conditions, or hold.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Checklist stresses without mechanisms. Collapsing environment exposure and life
projection. Using an EVT hero lot as the life population.

\subsection{Manufacturing validation}
\label{sec:ladder-production}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
The factory is part of the design. Prove you can build it repeatedly, measure
it repeatedly, and detect bad units. Qualification proves the design.
Manufacturing validation proves the process.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
A few carefully built engineering samples cannot establish volume readiness.
This is the Production Validation Test (\term{PVT}\firstterm{PVT}) question:
whether yield, process control, and ATP coverage survive lot-to-lot variation
(\Cref{sec:hvm-test,sec:supplier-exec,tab:npi}). Design Validation Test
(\term{DVT}\firstterm{DVT}) belongs earlier: it freezes corners, margin, and
the life plan before volume tooling. Do not park DVT inside this stage.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Qualified design intent; multi-lot material; ATE-to-bench correlation plan;
escape signatures from earlier stages.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
\begin{description}
\item[ATP] 100\% or classified screens that catch known escapes.
\item[SPC] Process monitoring on the variables that move yield.
\item[Audit] Periodic deep tests the ATP cannot afford every unit.
\item[Supplier controls] Incoming quality and FAIR gates.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Production data systems, golden units, and supplier lot genealogy. Engineering
lab access for correlation failures.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Lots, date codes, sites/lines, process corners, measurement capability, yield
confidence, guardband justification, escape-detection proof
(\Cref{sec:tree-evidence-block,sec:hvm-test}).

\paragraph{Analysis.}
For a proposed ATP or screen update that claims to catch prior escapes, prove
three things: replay the historical failing unit or equivalent; show separation
of good and bad; show production repeatability supports the limit. Split
supplier RMA codes so one vendor cannot hide inside a merged bucket.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} multi-lot yield, classified ATP/sample/SPC coverage, SPC
stability, and FAIR evidence support opening volume, or when you hold for
process control.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Open volume toward pilot, hold shipment, or demand corrective action before
ramp.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Shipping an uncontrolled second lot after a qualified hero process. Treating
ATP as a substitute for qualification. Silent ``multi-lot'' that means two
hand-selected lots.

\subsection{Controlled pilot}
\label{sec:ladder-fleet}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Validate assumptions in real deployment with a bounded population. Distinct
from mass production.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Lab and factory evidence may still miss install practice, traffic mix, or
environment.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Known serial numbers and lots; representative hosts and environments; enhanced
telemetry; success and rollback criteria; observation duration
(\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).

\paragraph{Measurements.}
Field BER/FEC and telemetry trends; install and reseat events; CMIS dumps
preserved before reseat; comparison to lab baselines.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Ops telemetry and RMA path. Bench FA when pilot units return.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Pilot cohort database, exit metrics, signatures that feed ATP or design rules.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Ask whether field reality matches lab assumptions. Feed mismatches back into
earlier stages.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} pilot success/rollback criteria are met, or when you
restrict or reject based on observed risk. Pilot has a real exit.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Expand deployment, restrict, pause a supplier or lot, or reopen an earlier
ladder stage.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Calling open volume a pilot. Learning the escape mechanism from customer
outage instead of from a bounded trial.

\subsection{Mass production}
\label{sec:ladder-mp}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Sustain volume with process control after pilot exit. Thin gate here; depth
lives in \Cref{tab:npi,ch:reliability}.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Pilot success does not prove ECO discipline, SPC stability, and RMA burn-down
at volume.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Pilot exit evidence; frozen ATP/SPC; supplier FAIR; ECO process.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
SPC trends, yield, RMA rates by code, ECO impact checks.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Manufacturing and supplier quality systems.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Volume KPI set with owners and reaction plans.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Hold or open volume based on control, not on hope that pilot luck continues.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} SPC, ECO, and RMA loops support sustained volume, or when
you hold.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Open / hold volume; trigger supplier corrective action.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Treating MP as fleet monitoring. Skipping ECO impact validation.

\subsection{Fleet monitoring}
\label{sec:ladder-fleet-monitor}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Validation does not end at shipment. Keep escapes and drift detectable after
release. Ongoing control, not a gate that exits into silence
(\Cref{sec:fleet-triage,tab:fleet-triage}).

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Unknown failure modes, aging, supplier drift, and environmental effects that
only appear at scale.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Schema-stable telemetry; serial/lot/firmware/platform identity; cohort query
tools; alarm ownership and retention policy.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
Per-lane power, bias, pre-FEC BER and FEC histograms; temperature and actuator
drive; LOS/LOL with context. Every telemetry field must answer: what decision
does this enable?

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Fleet telemetry and RMA. Bench FA for returns.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Cohort baselines, alarm history, RMA codes split by vendor and mechanism class.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Trend and disagreement alarms first; hard thresholds catch dead units, trends
catch dying ones.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
There is no terminal exit. \textbf{Ownership transfers} into steady operations
when schema, owners, and cohort baselines are in place and broader ship was
justified.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Continue ship, restrict, pause a supplier or lot, or reopen an earlier ladder
stage when cohort evidence falsifies a qual assumption.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Logging registers with no decision owner. Closing NFF without a reproduction
plan.

\subsection{Feedback / next revision}
\label{sec:ladder-feedback}

\paragraph{Purpose.}
Close the loop into the next requirements and architecture revision. Fleet and
FA evidence that does not change the product is wasted.

\paragraph{What uncertainty remains?}
Which escapes, margin erosions, or requirement gaps must reshape the next
design or screen set?

\paragraph{Inputs.}
Fleet cohorts, FA/DPA, supplier 8D, ATP escapes, pilot lessons
(\Cref{sec:systems-engineering-loop}).

\paragraph{Measurements.}
None new by default. Mine existing evidence for revision targets.

\paragraph{Required access level.}
Cross-functional: design, manufacturing, supplier, and fleet ops.

\paragraph{Data collected.}
Revision backlog with owners: requirement changes, architecture changes, ATP
updates, derates.

\paragraph{Analysis.}
Separate one-off install errors from systematic design or process debt.

\paragraph{Exit criteria.}
\textbf{Exit when} next-revision targets are written with owners, or when you
explicitly accept residual risk without change.

\paragraph{Decision enabled.}
Next-revision targets, ATP updates, or documented accept risk.

\paragraph{Common mistakes.}
Fleet tickets that never become requirements. Fixing units without changing
screens.

\subsection{Which evidence class answers the uncertainty?}
\label{sec:ladder-evidence-class}

The lifecycle says \emph{when}. This fork says \emph{which class of evidence}
to buy first for a named uncertainty:

\begin{dectree}
New product uncertainty
  |
What requirement is uncertain?
  |
Can simulation answer it?
  |-- YES --> Model validation, then confirm critical assumptions later
  |-- NO  --> Can bench measurement answer it?
              |-- YES --> Characterization / margin measurement
              |-- NO  --> Qualification stress
                          |
                          Field evidence (pilot / fleet) if lab cannot close it
\end{dectree}

Do not use this fork as a second lifecycle. Use it inside a stage when choosing
between model, bench, stress, and field evidence.

\subsection{Why the stages occur in this order}

Requirements and architecture prevent measuring the wrong product. Bring-up
makes later data interpretable. Characterization builds the behavioral model.
Margin measures remaining headroom; interoperability proves that headroom in
real systems. Reliability qualification answers life. Manufacturing validation
answers repeatability. Controlled pilot checks lab assumptions in a bounded
cohort; mass production sustains volume; fleet monitoring keeps escapes
visible; feedback writes the next revision.

Later stages must not compensate for incomplete earlier stages. A large
interoperability matrix cannot fix unstable bring-up. Reliability testing
cannot establish manufacturing consistency from one engineering lot. An HTOL
pass does not prove bring-up on the target host. Treat each exit as evidence
only for its own question (\Cref{tab:ladder,sec:tree-qualification}).

\section{The core IM/DD measurements}

Once the ladder is clear, the measurement list is organized around isolation:
transmitter, channel, and receiver. That split is older than PAM4. Long before
TDECQ, field engineers learned that a dark link can be a dead laser, a dirty
connector, or a dead TIA, and that guessing which one burns hours. Bisecting those
three domains is still how you keep debug from turning into simultaneous retunes
of everything.

\subsection{Transmitter}

Start with the light leaving the faceplate or the CPO fiber array. For PAM4, the
headline metric is \term{TDECQ}\firstterm{TDECQ} (transmitter and dispersion eye
closure quaternary): a reference equalizer is applied to the captured eye and the
residual penalty is reported in dB (\Cref{sec:tdecq}). Alongside it you read
\term{OMA}\firstterm{OMA} (outer), extinction ratio, and \term{RLM}\firstterm{RLM}
(level linearity), plus wavelength, spectral width, and RIN with a bias-driver
versus feedback bisect (\Cref{sec:laser-params,sec:laser-drivers,sec:rin-values}).

What else you add depends on the transmitter style. Laser-bearing modules need
LIV, threshold, slope, SMSR, and chirp checks for DMLs
(\Cref{sec:laser-params,sec:dfb-eml}). External MZMs (TFLN or silicon) need EO
$S_{21}$, $V_\pi$, quadrature bias versus temperature, and driver-path eye
symmetry at baud (\Cref{sec:simzm,sec:tfln-mzm,sec:tdecq}). Microring banks need
resonance alignment, thermal tuning, neighbor crosstalk, and peaking-network EO
$S_{21}$ (\Cref{sec:siring,ch:wdm}). The point of the list is not completeness for
its own sake: it is knowing which instrument answers which hypothesis when the
eye closes.

\subsection{Channel}
\label{sec:optical-channel}

If the transmitter looks clean into a golden receiver and the link still fails,
the channel is next. Insertion loss from fiber, connectors, MUX/de-MUX
(\Cref{sec:wdm-hardware}), and on-chip coupling (\Cref{sec:simzm,sec:siring}) is
the first ledger line. Use the specified maximum loss for the exact connector
class, number of interfaces, cleanliness condition, and reference plane; do not
treat ``1--3~dB per mated pair'' as a universal normal loss. Chromatic
dispersion (\Cref{sec:chirp-dispersion}) matters more on FR-class SMF sweeps than
on short DR links. Optical return loss (ORL) is the quiet killer: reflections can
create optical feedback noise, multipath interference, deterministic distortion,
and power-independent error floors. That is why many DR/FR modules still carry
isolators while some CPO engines rely on design margin and monitor photodiodes
instead (\Cref{sec:rin-values,ch:lasers}). Fiber attach
(MPO/MTP, FAU, grating couplers) shows up as both yield and reliability
(\Cref{sec:photonic-packaging}).

\subsection{Receiver}

Receiver work asks whether the front-end can still decide bits at the OMA that
survives the channel. Measure sensitivity (minimum OMA for the target BER) and
stressed-receiver sensitivity with a calibrated stressor for margin
(\Cref{sec:secq}), plus overload before the TIA saturates. Underneath those
system numbers sit the photodiode/TIA pair: responsivity, bandwidth, and
input-referred noise (\Cref{sec:pd-tia,ch:models}).

\subsection{Link level}

Only after Tx, channel, and Rx each look sane do you trust a full-link verdict:
pre-FEC BER against the KP4 threshold (\Cref{sec:kp4}), post-FEC BER, FEC
symbol-error histograms, and a signed link-budget ledger from transmitter OMA to
receiver sensitivity with penalties and remaining margin. That ledger is the
document you argue from in DVT; the BER alone is not.

\subsection{Reading the measurement map}

Use the table for lookup. Use the notes below when a metric is new, or when you
need the decision the measurement unlocks.

\paragraph{OMA / TDECQ.}
TDECQ asks how much worse this transmitter is than an ideal source after a
reference equalizer. Outer OMA is the optical swing the receiver actually uses.
Together they answer whether the Tx path still has signal-quality margin.
\textbf{Exit when} TDECQ and OMA meet the PMD/ATP at the named pattern and
temperature. \textbf{Decision:} continue, retune bias/equalization, or reject
the transmitter path. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} you chase receiver noise while
the eye was already out of budget.

\paragraph{Extinction ratio / RLM.}
Extinction ratio and level separation mismatch (RLM) set how much OMA you get
at fixed average power and how linear the PAM4 levels are. Poor RLM inflates
TDECQ even when average power looks fine. \textbf{Exit when} ER/RLM meet the
mask at the failing corner. \textbf{Decision:} retune modulator bias or
driver, or accept a derate. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} average-power APC hides
a collapsing outer eye.

\paragraph{Wavelength / SMSR.}
Wavelength placement and side-mode suppression ask whether the spectral ledger
still closes: on-grid for filters or rings, single-mode under temperature and
age. \textbf{Exit when} the line sits in the allowed window with SMSR in
spec. \textbf{Decision:} retune lock/thermal control, derate temperature, or
replace the laser. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} BER failures get blamed on RIN
when the line walked onto a filter edge.

\paragraph{RIN.}
Relative intensity noise sets how far $Q$ can rise with power. Measure with a
quiet bias path and under controlled ORL so you separate intrinsic laser noise
from feedback. \textbf{Exit when} RIN at the stated ORL meets the budget.
\textbf{Decision:} fix reflections/supply, replace the laser, or stop raising
launch into a floor. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} you keep adding photons to a
non-power-limited impairment (\Cref{sec:interview-waterfall}).

\paragraph{Insertion loss / ORL.}
Insertion loss is the first power-ledger line. ORL asks whether reflections
are seeding RIN or bursts. \textbf{Exit when} loss and ORL are inside the
plant assumptions used in the link budget. \textbf{Decision:} clean/replace
connectors, add isolation, or reopen the budget. \textbf{Risk if skipped:}
burst tickets look like random laser death.

\paragraph{Receiver sensitivity.}
Sensitivity is the minimum OMA for the target BER, the budget's bottom line.
A parallel waterfall shift with no floor usually means the Rx path or channel
loss changed. \textbf{Exit when} sensitivity meets the ledger with stated
pattern and stress. \textbf{Decision:} golden-swap ownership, derate reach, or
redesign Rx. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} Tx FA on an Rx-limited link.

\paragraph{Pre-FEC BER / FEC histogram.}
Pre-FEC BER is the system score every other metric feeds. The FEC histogram
shape separates sparse Gaussian-like errors from clustered bursts (MPI,
intermittents, unlocked intervals). \textbf{Exit when} BER and histogram
support the claimed mechanism class. \textbf{Decision:} contain, clean,
retune, or open FA. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} average BER hides a bursty
escape that ATP never stressed.

\usuallymeans{BER waterfall floor that more launch power does not fix}{RIN,
MPI, crosstalk, receiver saturation, or another non-power-limited impairment
(\Cref{sec:interview-waterfall})}{simple insertion loss that more photons will
buy out}

\paragraph{CMIS state / DDM.}
Management state and digital diagnostics confirm the control and monitor path
before you blame photons. Disagreement between digital diagnostics monitoring
(DDM) and an external meter is itself a finding (monitor-PD or calibration).
\textbf{Exit when} state progression and DDM match bench truth at the named
plane. \textbf{Decision:} fix firmware/seat/power, or proceed to optics.
\textbf{Risk if skipped:} weeks of optical FA on a module that never reached
ready.

\subsection{Why this map is ordered by isolation}

Transmitter metrics come before channel and receiver metrics because a bad Tx
eye contaminates every downstream number. Channel loss and ORL come before Rx
sensitivity arguments for the same reason. Link-level BER is last: it is the
verdict, not the first bisect. If you start at BER alone, you still need this
map to choose the next instrument.

\section{Transmitter and dispersion eye closure quaternary (TDECQ)}
\label{sec:tdecq}

\term{TDECQ} (transmitter and dispersion eye closure quaternary) deserves a closer
look because it is the metric that governs many PAM4 transmitter acceptance
methods. It answers a specific question: \emph{how much worse is this transmitter
than an ideal one, after a realistic receiver has done what it can to clean up the
signal?}

The following is a representative IEEE-style procedure. Use the exact clause and
PMD under qualification for the reference receiver, equalizer constraints,
histogram locations, and target error ratio.

\subsection{How it is measured}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Capture.} The optical waveform is acquired on a sampling
      oscilloscope (a DCA) through a standardized reference receiver (often a
      fourth-order Bessel--Thomson filter near half the baud rate under the named
      PMD) so every lab measures the same bandwidth.
\item \textbf{Equalize.} A defined \term{reference equalizer}, a
      \term{feed-forward equalizer}\firstterm{FFE} (FFE) with a small, bounded number of taps
      (commonly up to five in many PMDs), is applied. This models the modest
      equalization a real receiver would perform, so the transmitter is not
      penalized for \term{ISI}\firstterm{ISI} the system can remove anyway.
\item \textbf{Histogram.} Narrow vertical histogram windows are placed inside the
      symbol at the positions required by the clause. The noise distribution is
      evaluated at the three PAM4 decision thresholds.
\item \textbf{Compute.} The algorithm finds the RMS Gaussian noise
      $\sigma$ that, added to the equalized signal, would just reach the clause's
      target symbol error ratio (often near $4.8\times10^{-4}$ for KP4-class
      budgets). TDECQ is the ratio, in dB, of the noise an \emph{ideal}
      transmitter could tolerate to the noise \emph{this} transmitter can
      tolerate:
      \[
        \mathrm{TDECQ} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{ideal}}}
        {\sigma_{\text{measured}}}\right).
      \]
\end{enumerate}

A worse transmitter tolerates less added noise before failing, so
$\sigma_{\text{measured}}$ shrinks and TDECQ rises. Lower is better; the numeric
cap is PMD-specific.

\subsection{Related quantities and failure signatures}

\begin{description}
\item[SECQ] \firstterm{SECQ} the stressed-eye counterpart used on the receiver side under a
      named PMD, adding a calibrated stressor to test margin rather than
      transmitter quality alone. Distinguish SECQ from a general stressed-receiver
      sensitivity test. See \Cref{sec:secq}.
\item[RLM (relative level mismatch)] measures how evenly the four PAM4 levels are
      spaced; poor RLM (uneven levels) inflates TDECQ.
\end{description}

Because TDECQ folds several impairments into one number, the way it fails is
diagnostic: uneven levels point to modulator or driver linearity (RLM);
residual eye closure the equalizer cannot fix points to excess ISI or limited
bandwidth; a noise-limited result points to low OMA, RIN, or reflections. For
external MZMs (TFLN or silicon), also check EO $S_{21}$ bandwidth, $V_\pi$ and
bias quadrature drift with temperature, and RF return loss on the driver-to-modulator
path (\Cref{sec:simzm,sec:tfln-mzm,sec:siring}). This is
why \term{LPO}, which removes the module's own DSP, raises the stakes on
transmitter quality: there is less downstream equalization to hide behind, so
TDECQ-class metrics become even more central.

\section{SECQ and stressed-receiver testing}
\label{sec:secq}

\term{SECQ} (stressed eye closure quaternary) mirrors TDECQ on the \emph{receiver}
for a named PMD and clause: instead of scoring transmitter quality with a
reference equalizer, the test applies a calibrated optical stressor (attenuation,
ISI template, optional RIN) and asks how much margin remains before the receiver
hits that clause's target pre-FEC metric.

Stressed-receiver sensitivity and overload tests (\Cref{sec:sensitivity}) use the
same philosophy but are not automatically the same procedure as SECQ. Bracket the
operating OMA range with impairments the link will see in the field, and name the
PMD, FEC architecture, error model, metric, and test duration. For LPO, where the
module DSP is gone, stressed Rx margin on the host-side receiver
(\Cref{sec:equalization,sec:conditioning}) is as important as TDECQ on the
transmitter.

\section{Instruments}
\label{sec:instruments}

A failing PAM4 link rarely announces which block is wrong. The bench is how you
force the answer: each instrument isolates one failure mode, and the loopback
topology tells you which side of the optical connector owns it.

\begin{description}
\item[DCA] \firstterm{DCA} (digital communication analyzer): sampling scope for PAM4 eyes, TDECQ,
      OMA, RLM (\Cref{sec:tdecq}). Needs a reference receiver filter matched to the
      PHY under test.
\item[BERT]\firstterm{BERT} bit-error ratio at pre- and post-FEC; FEC symbol histograms
      (\Cref{sec:kp4}).
\item[OSA / wavemeter]\firstterm{OSA} wavelength, spectrum, SMSR, side modes, and linewidth where
      supported (\Cref{ch:lasers}).
\item[PD + ESA or dedicated RIN analyzer] relative intensity-noise spectrum under a
      defined condition (\Cref{sec:rin-values}).
\item[VOA / stressor assembly]\firstterm{VOA} calibrated attenuation and optional ISI for SECQ and
      sensitivity sweeps.
\item[Power meter] average power; pair with DCA for OMA.
\item[Thermal chamber + TEC controller] corner validation; essential for rings
      (\Cref{sec:siring,ch:wdm}) and laser grids.
\end{description}

Use electrical loopback (host SerDes), optical loopback (Tx$\to$Rx on module), and
golden-host/golden-module interop to bisect faults (\Cref{sec:optical-channel}).
If the fault follows the module under golden-host swap, stop blaming the SerDes;
if it stays with the host, stop opening laser FA.

\section{Building a link budget}
\label{sec:link-budget}

A link budget is a signed dB (or power) ledger from transmitter to receiver.
For IM/DD short reach, start from outer OMA at the Tx faceplate and subtract every
loss and penalty until you compare against receiver sensitivity (with target BER
and KP4 pre-FEC threshold, \Cref{sec:kp4,sec:sensitivity}).

\begin{dectree}
Transmitter output (OMA)
  |
Coupling loss
  |
Connector loss
  |
Fiber / waveguide loss
  |
Penalties (Method A or B; see below)
  |
Receiver input
  |
Sensitivity requirement
  |
Remaining margin
\end{dectree}

Keep power budget, signal-quality penalties, timing, thermal, and control
authority as separate ledgers when the impairment is not a pure optical-power
number (\Cref{sec:laser-margin-erosion,sec:tree-margin-budget}).

\paragraph{Design allocation versus validation measurement.}
Distinguish margin allocation in design from margin verification in test.
During design, engineers allocate transmitter output, receiver sensitivity,
insertion loss, temperature degradation, aging, jitter, and manufacturing
variation. During customer or system qualification, the integrator often
validates the net behavior across the operating envelope.

\begin{dectree}
Design: allocate line items
  |
Build / integrate
  |
Test: measure net at named plane
  |
Room-T sensitivity margin
  |
Temperature / stress sweep
  |
Observed margin loss
  |
Remaining headroom
  |
Deployment decision
\end{dectree}

\paragraph{TDECQ accounting: two methods.}
\begin{description}
\item[Method A --- Composite compliance] Use the PMD's specified OMA/TDECQ
      relationship. Do not subtract TDECQ again as an independent link-budget
      penalty.
\item[Method B --- Engineering decomposition] Use a separate measured
      transmitter-quality penalty only when the accounting method is defined and
      does not duplicate the compliance limit.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Illustrative ledger (single-mode DR-class sketch).}
Start from Tx OMA on the DCA (or from average power and ER) at a named plane.
Subtract connector/coupling loss using the specified maximum for the connector
class, interface count, and cleanliness (an illustrative poor or multi-interface
allocation can land near 1--3~dB per mated pair; that is not a universal normal
loss). Subtract fiber loss ($\sim$0.3--0.4~dB/km at 1310~nm; often negligible at
500~m) and MUX/de-MUX if WDM (2--5~dB per stage, \Cref{sec:wdm-hardware}). Apply
penalties with Method~A or Method~B above; add dispersion
(\Cref{sec:chirp-dispersion}) and reflection/MPI terms
(\Cref{sec:optical-channel,sec:rin-values}) only when not already absorbed. Compare
the remainder to stressed sensitivity at the \emph{named} PMD's pre-FEC objective
(for a KP4-class optical PMD under its random-error model, often near
$2.4\times10^{-4}$; state FEC, metric, and test duration). Keep production margin
appropriate to fleet corners. Numbers here are an illustrative DR-class sketch,
not universal limits.
Electrical budgets parallel this for the host-to-module path: COM and pre-FEC BER
(\Cref{sec:com,sec:eye-budget,sec:equalization}). LPO requires \emph{both} ledgers
to close without module DSP help.

\subsection{What CMIS is, and why an optical engineer cares}

\term{CMIS}\firstterm{CMIS} (Common Management Interface Specification) is the
vendor-neutral management layer between a host (switch ASIC, NIC, or test fixture)
and a pluggable or on-board optical module that implements it. The host talks to
the module over a two-wire bus (TWI, I2C-like) through a paged register map:
identity, power mode, alarms, per-lane monitors, and (in later revisions)
link-training and host signal-integrity tuning extensions~\cite{cmis53}. Common
form factors include QSFP-DD, OSFP, COBO, ELSFP, and some CPO engines that expose
a CMIS contract.

You touch CMIS on every bring-up and every field triage. It is how the host learns
what module is seated, when lasers may turn on, what Tx/Rx power and temperature
look like, and whether a link failed at the management layer or the optical layer.
A module that passes BER on a bench with lasers forced on but cannot reach
ModuleReady on a production host will fail in the fleet (\Cref{sec:bringup}).

\subsection{The module state machine}

CMIS defines a module state machine the host drives. After presence detect and
power application, the module stays in low power until the host releases
\texttt{LPModeL} (or the CMIS 5.x \texttt{LowPwr} equivalent). The host reads
identifier pages, clears sticky interrupts, and steps the module toward
ModuleReady. Only then should Tx lanes or ELS lasers enable. ELSFP modules that
emit before ModuleReady are a reject: the host did not authorize light
(\Cref{sec:elsfp}).

Data paths have their own state machines in CMIS 5.x (data path states, and
network path states for media-side links). For bring-up, map the sequence in
\Cref{sec:bringup} onto these transitions: presence and Vcc, CMIS init and
ModuleReady, enable light, optical path check, electrical lock, traffic, snapshot.
Skipping step 2 and jumping to BER is how interop failures hide until production.

\subsection{The memory map: pages, monitors, control}

The lower memory map holds module identity, status, interrupt flags, and alarm
thresholds. Upper pages hold application descriptors, lane controls, tunable-laser
support, versatile diagnostics (VDM), and command-data-block (CDB) firmware
messaging~\cite{cmis53}. Hosts select an application (lane count, host interface,
media type) before bringing up traffic.

\term{DDM}\firstterm{DDM} (digital diagnostic monitoring) is the telemetry layer
you read at scale: per-lane Tx and Rx optical power, laser bias current when
exposed, module temperature, supply voltage, LOS/LOL flags, and alarm/warning
bits. On WDM parts you also get wavelength or channel ID. This is exactly what
\Cref{sec:fleet-triage} reads before anyone reaches for a DCA. On bring-up, dump
the register map you will use in the field and treat that dump as the golden
reference for later RMA comparisons.

\subsection{CMIS as a validation deliverable}

CMIS correctness is part of production readiness, not a firmware afterthought.
ATP should prove the state machine reaches ModuleReady across voltage and thermal
corners; DDM monitors track bench truth (CMIS Tx power versus DCA, module
temperature versus case $T$); alarms fire at the right thresholds; and firmware
revision is ECO-controlled like laser die revision (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec}).
Multi-source interop failures are often CMIS, media-type, or firmware mismatches,
not marginal TDECQ (\Cref{sec:bringup}). At fleet scale the register map is the
only eyes you have on a module in the rack. If CMIS is wrong, triage starts blind.

\section{Module and system bring-up}
\label{sec:bringup}

Characterization proves a sample can meet metrics on a quiet bench. Bring-up
proves a module (then a system) can be powered, managed, and linked the way
production and the fleet will actually run it. Lab-to-production programs fail in
the gap between those two if you only ever test golden hosts, clean fiber, and
room-temperature faceplates.

\paragraph{Module bring-up sequence.}
Run this order on every new module (pluggable, ELSFP, or CPO engine with CMIS).
Do not skip ahead to BER: a link that ``works'' with lasers forced on and CMIS
ignored will fail the first host that enforces the state machine
(\Cref{sec:elsfp}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Presence and power.} Detect module (\texttt{ModPrsL} or equivalent).
      Apply rails in the host power sequence. Confirm Vcc and module temperature
      in CMIS. Stay in low power (\texttt{LPModeL} asserted or ModuleLowPwr) until
      management is sane.
\item \textbf{CMIS init.} Read identifier, vendor, firmware rev, supported media.
      Clear sticky interrupts. Confirm the state machine can reach ModuleReady
      (or the pluggable equivalent) under host command. Dump the register map you
      will use in the field; that dump is your bring-up golden reference.
\item \textbf{Enable light.} Exit low power; enable Tx lanes / ELS lasers only after
      ModuleReady. Confirm Tx optical power and laser bias (if exposed) against the
      power class. Lasers that come up before the host asks are a reject for ELSFP
      (\Cref{sec:elsfp}).
\item \textbf{Optical path.} Mate fiber (clean first). Check Rx power and
      LOS\firstterm{LOS}. Optical loopback first if the host path is unproven.
\item \textbf{Electrical lock.} Bring host SerDes / module CDR. Confirm
      LOL\firstterm{LOL} clear, equalizer taps not pegged (\Cref{sec:equalization}).
      For LPO, this is the host eye and COM path (\Cref{sec:com,sec:drivers}).

\subsection{Reading the production-corner map}
\label{sec:prod-corners-read}

Quiet $T$/$V$ characterization maps the part. \Cref{tab:prod-corners} asks
whether that map survives rack, host, plant, and service abuse. Use the table
for the full set. The notes below teach the two corners that most often fool
a quiet bench; the rest are Exit/Decision only.

\paragraph{Chassis thermal (worked).}
Chamber case-$T$ does not prove sled airflow or faceplate gradient. Run the
module in the target rack at production load; log case $T$, TEC current,
lock, and pre-FEC BER versus the chamber baseline. \textbf{Exit when} loaded
thermal closes with margin or names a derate / TEC / unlock restriction.
\textbf{Decision:} approve the envelope, restrict deployment, or redesign
cooling. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} quiet-chamber passes unlock in the first
dense tray.

\paragraph{LPO / linear path (worked).}
Retimed modules hide host FIR and module linearity faults
(\Cref{sec:224g-deploy,sec:com,sec:drivers}). Run host COM and pre-FEC BER on
the linear path. \textbf{Exit when} BER and COM meet targets on the
production host, or LPO is rejected for that host class. \textbf{Decision:}
approve LPO, force retimed optics, or redesign host FIR / module linearity.
\textbf{Risk if skipped:} LPO ships on hope and fails on the production ASIC
SerDes.

\paragraph{Other corners (retrieve from the table).}
\begin{description}
\item[Host rails live] \textbf{Exit when} BER and bias telemetry stay clean
      under host supplies with traffic. \textbf{Decision:} approve pairing or
      demand PSRR/ground work. \textbf{Risk:} chasing optical RIN for host
      noise.
\item[Dirty fiber / ORL] \textbf{Exit when} stressed-ORL BER meets the plant
      budget or forces isolator / cleaning rules. \textbf{Decision:} approve
      plant practice or tighten service. \textbf{Risk:} lab heroes fail the
      first dirty install.
\item[Cable plant] \textbf{Exit when} production fiber/MPO/bend closes the
      signed budget. \textbf{Decision:} approve plant or cut reach.
      \textbf{Risk:} budget fiction on long MPO chains.
\item[ELS hot-swap] \textbf{Exit when} swap recovers to ready and BER, or
      service is restricted (\Cref{sec:elsfp}). \textbf{Decision:} approve
      field replace or forbid hot-swap. \textbf{Risk:} service story fails
      the first maintenance window.
\item[Neighbor load] \textbf{Exit when} full-traffic neighbors close lock and
      BER. \textbf{Decision:} approve dense packing or derate. \textbf{Risk:}
      single-module DVT passes; tray bring-up fails.
\item[Voltage corners] \textbf{Exit when} host Vcc min/max holds CMIS and BER.
      \textbf{Decision:} approve envelope or tighten ATP. \textbf{Risk:}
      brown-outs look like firmware bugs.
\end{description}

representative unit. The full set belongs before PVT exit
(\Cref{tab:npi}). Later fleet monitoring must not invent coverage these
corners never ran.

\subsection{Learning summary}

\begin{description}
\item[Before DVT] Chassis thermal, host rails, and ORL on a representative unit.
\item[Before PVT] Full \Cref{tab:prod-corners} set, including LPO if claimed.
\item[Each corner] Exit when the claim closes or a restriction is named.
\end{description}

\paragraph{System bring-up.}
\tradeoff{Best laboratory performance vs production yield}{Hero samples that win
bench demos}{Tighter tolerances, harder calibration, and escapes in volume}{When
the manufacturable design still meets the system requirement with guardband}{Optimize
the system and the yield story, not the best component on a quiet bench.}

A module that passes on a golden host can still fail in a real chassis:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Host path:} run the same sequence on the target NIC/switch ASIC SerDes,
      not only the lab BERT. LPO and half-retimed modules expose host FIR/CTLE
      mistakes that a retimed module hid (\Cref{sec:conditioning,sec:pluggables}).
\item \textbf{Multi-lane / multi-module:} bring all lanes on a port, then neighbors
      in the same cage or tray. Watch thermal rise, supply droop, and CMIS temp
      alarms when the tray is loaded.
\item \textbf{Golden swap:} known-good module in the suspect host slot, then suspect
      module in a known-good slot. That single swap splits host vs.\ module before
      you open FA (\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).
\item \textbf{Interop:} at least one other vendor host or module if the program
      claims multi-source. Interop failures are usually CMIS, media type, or
      electrical eye, not laser physics.
\item \textbf{ELS / CPO:} external laser modules add a second bring-up: ELSFP state
      machine and optical mate to the engine, then engine bring-up with light
      present (\Cref{sec:elsfp,sec:cpo-status}). A dark engine with a healthy ELS is
      an optical connector or FAU problem until proven otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Exit criteria before ``bring-up done.''}
Call \emph{bench bring-up} done when CMIS state machine and enable sequence are
correct, the unit emits and receives light in class, CDR locks, pre-FEC BER is
usable on a trusted setup at a named plane, and a CMIS+BER+$T$ snapshot is filed.
Call \emph{system integration bring-up} done when the same sequence closes on the
target host, golden-swap has split host vs.\ module issues, and multi-lane /
neighbor load has not opened a new basic failure mode. Do \emph{not} require
loaded chassis-thermal / host-rail / ORL margin closure to declare bring-up
done; that is Stage~3 margin and interop evidence
(\Cref{sec:ladder-margin,sec:ladder-interop,tab:prod-corners}). Everything after bring-up is
characterization depth, margin/interop, supplier gates
(\Cref{sec:supplier-exec}), or fleet triage (\Cref{sec:fleet-triage}).

\keyidea{Bring-up is a sequence (presence $\to$ CMIS $\to$ light $\to$ lock $\to$
BER $\to$ snapshot), then a system proof on the real host. Production-representative
corners prove remaining headroom; they do not redefine bench bring-up. A quiet
bench pass is not DVT.}

\section{The debug mindset}
\label{sec:debug}

Debug at this level is data-driven, not opinion-driven. The method is disciplined
bisection: change one domain at a time, and let the measurement tell you whether
the transmitter, the channel, or the receiver moved.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Isolate transmitter versus channel versus receiver, using loopbacks.
\item Sweep temperature and voltage to expose corner-dependent failures.
\item Correlate failures to DSP equalizer tap values (\Cref{sec:equalization}) and FEC symbol-error
      statistics (\Cref{sec:kp4}); these tell you \emph{how} the link fails.
\end{enumerate}

The third step is where modern PAM4 links differ from older eye-mask work. Tap
saturation and FEC histograms often reveal the failure mode before a single
waveform screenshot does. Treat those as primary evidence, not as afterthoughts
logged once BER already fails.

\aside{Worked example. ``The link fails only at high temperature.'' Candidate
causes: laser wavelength drift off the grid or off a ring resonance; TEC
saturation; EAM bias shift; elevated receiver thermal noise; LIV rollover
(\Cref{sec:laser-aging,sec:laser-params}). You bisect with
measurements (spectrum for wavelength, LIV for the laser, receiver sensitivity
at temperature) rather than guessing.}

\section{The debugging fork in validation}
\label{sec:validation-fork}

Apply the debugging fork (\Cref{sec:debug-fork}) before sweeping parameters or
changing firmware: check the power meter or CMIS Rx power monitor first. If power
moved, the fault is in the optical path (laser, coupling, connector, fiber, MUX);
if power held but BER or TDECQ worsened, it is signal quality (bandwidth, noise,
jitter, bias, equalization, reflection). This one check prevents the most common
validation mistake: retuning an equalizer or laser bias when the real cause is a
dirty connector. Then check which margin ledger moved
(\Cref{sec:laser-margin-erosion}) before descending to component physics.

\whyexp{separate power from quality first?}{Because average optical power is
cheap to measure and rules out gross attenuation, but it says almost nothing
about timing, noise, distortion, spectral alignment, or adaptation.}

\usuallymeans{Stable average power with rising BER}{timing, adaptation, noise,
spectral alignment, or intermittent control}{gross attenuation or a simple dirty
connector as the whole story}

\heuristic{Never spend an hour on a DCA or spectrum sweep when a five-minute
golden swap or attenuator step can eliminate half the tree.}

\begin{dectree}

  |

  |

  |

  |

  |

\end{dectree}

\section{Fleet and field triage}
\label{sec:fleet-triage}

Lab debug asks: \emph{what is broken on this unit?} Fleet triage asks:
\emph{which bucket does this failure belong in, and who owns the fix?} Optical
programs at fleet scale own that split across performance, reliability, and
manufacturability. Wrong bucket wastes weeks (sending a contaminated connector to
laser FA, or rewriting a SerDes FIR when the laser is rolling over).

\heuristic{Contain the population and clear the measurement system before you
open supplier FA. A wrong ticket burns calendar time you cannot get back.}

\usuallymeans{Temperature-only failures that recover cool}{thermal margin,
wavelength or lock drift, bias tables, receiver noise rise, or mechanics that
move with case temperature}{a permanent wear-out mechanism already proven by
ship LIV alone}

\tradeoff{More telemetry vs operational complexity}{Faster fleet debug, better
cohort plots, earlier prediction}{Firmware cost, storage, and interpretation
burden}{When each new field answers a named decision}{Every telemetry field needs
an owner and a decision it enables. Otherwise it is noise.}

\paragraph{Three buckets.}
Classify every field issue before deep root-cause work:

\begin{description}
\item[Performance] the design or operating point does not close the budget under
      the conditions seen in the fleet. Examples: TDECQ/RLM marginal at case
      temperature, host COM tight on LPO, ring unlock under thermal crosstalk,
      ORL-driven RIN that the architecture assumed away. Fix is usually retune,
      derate, firmware, or a design/spec change (\Cref{sec:tdecq,sec:com,sec:siring}).
\item[Reliability] the unit met spec at ship and later degraded. Examples: LIV
      threshold rise, SMSR collapse, EAM bias creep, COD, TEC wear, epoxy creep on
      fiber attach. Fix is Arrhenius-backed life projection, burn-in/screen, derating,
      or field-replaceable lasers
      (\Cref{sec:wearout-modes,sec:laser-aging,sec:gr468,sec:elsfp}).
\item[Manufacturability] a subpopulation fails early or never met the ATP; the
      issue tracks lot, date code, supplier site, or assembly step. Examples: FAU
      misalign yield cliff, solder void on a driver die attach, incoming DPPM spike,
      CMIS register map mismatch on one firmware rev. Fix is SPC, ATP tighten,
      first-article, DPA, and 8D/CAPA with the supplier
      (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec,sec:photonic-packaging}).
\end{description}

A single symptom can sit in more than one bucket until you bisect. The tree below
forces the split with telemetry first, then a short bench confirm, then an RMA
label. \Cref{ch:failure-modes} expands the same method into symptom-led bench and
fleet procedures.

\paragraph{Telemetry you actually read.}
At scale you rarely start with a DCA. Start with what the host and module already
report:

\begin{itemize}
\item \term{CMIS} monitors and alarms: module temperature, supply
      rails, Tx/Rx optical power, laser bias (when exposed), wavelength or channel
      ID on WDM parts, LOS/LOL flags, and interrupt history (\texttt{IntL} on ELSFP;
      \Cref{sec:elsfp}).
\item Host link state: CDR lock, pre-FEC BER, FEC symbol-error histogram shape
      (\Cref{sec:kp4}), equalizer tap saturation (\Cref{sec:equalization}).
\item Fleet context: rack position, case temperature, time since install, date
      code / lot, neighbor-link correlation (one bad fiber vs whole tray).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reading the fleet triage map}

Each row is a provisional route, not a confirmed root cause. Capture telemetry
first. Confirm with the smallest measurement that can falsify the bucket. Then
assign an owner.

\paragraph{Link never comes up (fresh install).}
Ask whether the part is seated, powered, and managed before you open laser FA.
CMIS presence, supply rails, Tx power flatline, and loss-of-signal (LOS) split
install from product. Confirm with visual fiber/connector checks, a golden
module swap, and a frozen CMIS dump. \textbf{Decision:} ops fix, or supplier
ATP if the fail tracks a lot. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} manufacturing escapes
get filed as design defects.

\paragraph{Intermittent LOS / burst errors.}
Ask whether the plant is reflecting or contaminating. Rx power dropouts and
bursty FEC histograms point at connectors or ORL before intrinsic RIN. Confirm
with inspect/clean, ORL meter, and RIN versus ORL. \textbf{Decision:} cleaning
discipline or packaging FA if RMAs repeat. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} burst
tickets become endless laser replacements.

\paragraph{Pre-FEC BER high, power OK.}
Power held, so leave the power ledger. Tap saturation, logged TDECQ/RLM, and
case temperature point at signal quality or host SI. Confirm on a DCA and with
host channel operating margin (COM) thinking on linear paths.
\textbf{Decision:} host SI, module Tx design, or retune. \textbf{Risk if
skipped:} you reseat fiber forever on a quality-path fail.

\paragraph{BER rises only at high case $T$.}
Ask whether the operating point or the device changed with heat. Telemetry for
temp alarms, Tx sag, and wavelength walk comes first. Confirm with LIV at
temperature, OSA, TEC/heater codes, and modulator bias. Cool-down recovery
raises $P(\mathrm{operating\ point})$; permanent shift raises
$P(\mathrm{aging})$. \textbf{Decision:} derate, thermal design, or supplier
life action. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} ambient-only debug misses the spent
control ledger.

\paragraph{Slow BER creep over weeks/months.}
Ask whether wear-out is spending the noise or power ledger. Bias current
rising at fixed Tx power raises $P(\mathrm{aging})$. Confirm against ship
ATP baselines (LIV, SMSR, power, spectrum) and lot history; recovery after
recalibration raises $P(\mathrm{control/cal})$ instead. \textbf{Decision:}
replace, derate, or update burn-in. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} FIT models stay
optimistic until the fleet teaches you.

\paragraph{Sudden hard fail, was healthy.}
Ask whether the event is catastrophic optical damage, ESD, or a shared
infrastructure hit. Neighbor links and the last good CMIS snapshot matter.
Confirm with dark LIV and physical FA; check date-code clusters.
\textbf{Decision:} FA plus supplier 8D, or infrastructure fix.
\textbf{Risk if skipped:} one COD event becomes a false process CAPA.

\paragraph{One date code / site fails early.}
Ask whether this is a manufacturing subpopulation. Lot Pareto and burn-in
escape rate are the first cuts. Confirm incoming SPC versus ATP and FA on a
sample. \textbf{Decision:} quarantine, CAPA, hold shipment. \textbf{Risk if
skipped:} a bad lot keeps deploying while FA studies one unit.

\paragraph{WDM / ring unlock, power OK.}
Ask whether the spectral or lock ledger was spent while average power looked
fine. Channel ID, neighbor thermal, and lock-loop status are the telemetry.
Confirm resonance tune, crosstalk, and line power. \textbf{Decision:} lock
firmware or thermal design. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} unlocks get mislabeled
as random BER.

\paragraph{ELSFP swap restores link.}
Ask whether the external laser, the connector, or the engine owned the fail.
Compare old versus new CMIS and connector cycles. Confirm MT inspect and LIV
on the returned module. \textbf{Decision:} split RMA codes for laser versus
connector. \textbf{Risk if skipped:} FIT burns down the wrong wear-out mode
(\Cref{sec:elsfp}).

\subsection{Why triage order matters}

Scope before mechanism. Telemetry before destructive FA. Bucket before owner.
Confirm before CAPA. Closing the loop into ATP is part of the incident, not
optional paperwork. Reversing that order produces NFF piles and merged RMA
codes that make life models dishonest.

\paragraph{How to walk an incident (order of operations).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Stabilize and capture.} Freeze CMIS dump, host BER/FEC counters, rack
      $T$, and install age before anyone reseats the module. Reseating destroys
      connector evidence.
\item \textbf{Localize.} One link vs tray vs rack. Tray-wide points at power,
      cooling, or a shared ELS. Single-link points at that module, fiber, or host
      lane.
\item \textbf{Classify} with \Cref{tab:fleet-triage}. Write the bucket on the
      ticket before FA starts.
\item \textbf{Confirm} with the smallest measurement that can falsify the bucket
      (golden swap, clean/inspect, LIV, TDECQ, ORL). Do not skip to DPA.
\item \textbf{Act.}
      \begin{itemize}
      \item Performance: change operating policy (derate, FIR, lock loop) or open a
            design/spec defect.
      \item Reliability: replace (ELSFP hot-swap when available), update FIT burn-down,
            tighten burn-in or derate (\Cref{sec:laser-aging,sec:fit-example}).
      \item Manufacturability: quarantine lot, incoming hold, supplier 8D with DPA
            photos and ATP deltas (\Cref{sec:supplier-exec}).
      \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Close the loop.} Feed the signature back into ATP and CMIS alarm
      thresholds so the next incident trips earlier.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Worked paths (three common tickets).}

\emph{``High temp only.''}
CMIS shows module near thermal limit and Tx power sagging. Bucket starts as
performance (thermal design / derate). A permanent LIV or spectrum shift at
temperature that matches an aged lot raises $P(\mathrm{aging})$ and justifies
moving the ticket toward reliability; cool-down recovery without baseline
shift keeps it in performance. Measure OSA wavelength before blaming the
laser: a ring unlock is still performance (\Cref{sec:siring,ch:wdm}).

\emph{``Random burst errors, average power fine.''}
Check FEC histogram for clustered errors and CMIS for Rx power dropouts. Clean
and measure ORL. If RIN rises with ORL, it is performance/architecture
(feedback). If ORL is fine and bursts track a date code, it is mfg (intermittent
fiber attach). If bursts grow over months at fixed ORL, suspect laser or driver
aging (\Cref{sec:laser-drivers,sec:laser-aging}).

\emph{``ELSFP replace fixed it; returned module looks alive on the bench.''}
Alive LIV with high ORL sensitivity or dirty MT face means connector/ORL
(mfg/ops), not laser death. Dead or kinked LIV means reliability. Split those RMA
codes explicitly or your FIT math will blame the wrong wear-out mode
(\Cref{sec:elsfp,sec:photonic-packaging}).

\paragraph{RMA labels that keep FIT honest.}
RMA\firstterm{RMA} codes should be distinct, not a single ``optics fail'':
\begin{itemize}
\item laser wear-out (LIV/SMSR/EAM baselines support aging; not proof alone);
\item COD / sudden dark;
\item connector / contamination / ORL;
\item fiber attach / FAU;
\item driver / bias electronics;
\item host / SerDes / LPO eye (not module);
\item NFF\firstterm{NFF} (no-fault-found; track these; high NFF means bad triage).
\end{itemize}
NFF rate and lot Pareto are as important as FIT. A rising NFF with clean LIV
points at install practice or intermittent connectors, not Arrhenius.

\section{Engineering lens}
\label{sec:validation-engineering-lens}

\subsection{How it works}

Validation is a chain of evidence, not a single pass: a number means nothing
without its reference plane, its corner, and its method. The chapter's ladder,
instruments, and triage tree are that evidence chain from bench to fleet.

\subsection{How it is measured}
\label{sec:validation-how-measured}

Use the least complex instrument that can falsify the current hypothesis.
\Cref{tab:measurement-mapping} maps every key metric to its instrument, rationale,
and failure signature in one lookup; the bring-up sequence
(\Cref{sec:bringup,sec:prod-corners}) orders those instruments into a workflow.

\subsection{How it fails}
\label{sec:validation-how-fails}

Validation fails when the setup, sample, or acceptance rule does not match the
product. Common misses are a stale calibration, the wrong reference plane, a
golden host that hides interop risk, pristine fiber that hides ORL sensitivity,
short BER dwell, one lane tested without neighbors, and chamber temperature used
as a substitute for measured case temperature. These are test escapes even when
the device physics is sound.

\failuremode{Low optical power}{A lane is dark or below its launch-power limit.}{A
laser or enable fault, coupling loss, connector contamination, fiber polarity,
calibration error, or a power-meter setup mistake.}{Known source and meter,
inspection scope, CMIS state and bias, power at successive planes, and a golden
fiber or module.}{Correct the setup first, then repair the failing source, attach,
connector, or control path. Add the signature at the earliest production test
that can catch it.}

\subsection{How it is debugged}
\label{sec:validation-how-debugged}

Preserve the failing state and record software, firmware, calibration, fixture,
cables, temperature, and supply. Verify the meter with a known source. Walk from
power to spectrum to waveform to BER, moving one reference plane at a time. Use a
golden swap to split host, module, and fiber. Only then stress temperature, voltage,
ORL, and neighbors. Every corrective action needs a repeated failing test, a
repeated passing test, and a guard against recurrence in ATP or telemetry.

\debugstory{A new module lot showed low optical power on one station.}{The same
units passed on a second station. A known source exposed an offset in the first
power-meter path.}{The lot was good, and the station was reading low.}{A reference
jumper had been replaced without updating the path-loss calibration.}{The station
was recalibrated, jumper identity was placed under change control, and a start-of-
shift source check was added.}

\section{Interview takeaway}
\label{sec:validation-design-review}

\keyidea{Validation is a chain of evidence. Start with calibrated power and
management state, move through spectrum and waveform, then trust BER only after
the blocks and reference planes are known. Run the target host, chassis, fiber,
and neighbor corners before calling the product ready.}

\paragraph{Three questions to test yourself.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Why is a passing BER on a golden bench not sufficient for production
      readiness?
\item BER is high but optical power looks fine. Apply the debugging fork
      (\Cref{sec:validation-fork}): what do you check next?
\item How do you detect tester drift before it becomes a yield cliff or a field
      escape?
\end{enumerate}
